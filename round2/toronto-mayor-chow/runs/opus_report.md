# Toronto 2026 Mayoral Election Forecast: Olivia Chow Holds a Narrow but Durable Edge Over Brad Bradford in a Two-Way Plurality Race

> The simulation projects a tight head-to-head contest in which incumbent Olivia Chow is the modest favourite, at roughly 62% to win. That edge rests on stronger advance-vote signals and a higher ceiling in the late polls, while split polling keeps an upset by Brad Bradford a real possibility.

---

## Projected Outcome: A Close Two-Way Race Leaning Chow

The simulation's central projection is a close, binary contest that favours the incumbent. Across the modelled outcomes, Olivia Chow wins the October 26, 2026 vote roughly **62% of the time**, and Brad Bradford wins the remaining ~38%. The edge does not come from a commanding lead. It comes from an accumulation of small advantages, and the polling evidence is split almost exactly down the middle.

**The baseline: a race that tightened into a coin-flip on the headline numbers**

The simulated campaign begins with Chow comfortably ahead and ends with the field compressed. Early polling put her clear of her challenger:

> "Liaison Strategies' IVR poll, fielded September 4–5, 2026, with n=1,000, put Olivia Chow at 50% and Brad Bradford at 39%."

> "Nanos Research's phone and online poll for CP24/CTV, fielded September 30–October 4, 2026, with n=755, reported decided-voter support of 52.4% for Olivia Chow, 42.6% for Brad Bradford, and 4% for Alexander."

After Chris Alexander's October 6 withdrawal made the race a straight two-way fight, the gap closed quickly. The simulation's own summary of the polling record states that "Olivia Chow's lead narrowed sharply in October 2026." Nanos tracked the erosion directly:

> "Nanos's poll found Chow's lead shrank from about 10 points on September 30–October 4, 2026, to about 3 points on October 15–18, 2026."

By the final week, the late polls offered no consensus:

> "Among six news-reported polls fielded October 11–21, 2026, three showed Olivia Chow ahead by 3 to 11 points and three showed Bradford ahead by 1 to 4 points."

On the Bradford side of that split, Mainstreet reported "Brad Bradford at 48.9% to Olivia Chow's 47%" (October 16–18), Liaison reported "Brad Bradford at 49% to Olivia Chow's 47%" (October 18–19), and Build Canada "had Brad Bradford narrowly ahead of Chow," at 48% to 44%. On the Chow side, Pollara measured "Chow at 53% to Bradford's 46%" (October 13–19), and Nanos had her at "49.8% to 46.6%" (October 15–18).

**Why the projection leans Chow despite the split**

The Chow-favoured outcome comes from three features of the late data that matter more than the topline average.

1. **Her ceiling is higher than Bradford's.** Bradford's best results cluster in a narrow band, ahead by 1 to 4 points. Chow's leads run from 3 to as many as 11 points. When the simulation weighs these asymmetric distributions, Chow's win scenarios include larger, more decisive margins. Bradford's win scenarios are mostly narrow.

2. **The most committed and already-voted electorate breaks her way.** This is the strongest "advance-vote signal" in the simulated record. Nanos splits the electorate by certainty:

> "Nanos found Olivia Chow ahead by 4 points among the most certain voters in its October 15–18, 2026 poll."

> "Nanos found Brad Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters in its October 15–18, 2026 poll."

Polls that include voters who have already cast ballots also favour Chow:

> "Pollara's October 23, 2026 release reported Chow at 55% to Bradford's 44%, counting respondents who had already voted."

This matters because a large share of the vote was banked before the late tightening: "A record 222,550 people voted in advance polls for the October 26, 2026 Toronto mayoral election from October 6 to October 11, 2026." Bradford's late gains therefore act on a smaller pool of remaining voters. His strength among less certain voters makes his path depend heavily on turnout.

3. **Her personal standing remains stronger.** Even as the horse race narrowed, the simulated electorate rated Chow more favourably on character and issues:

> "Pollara's October 23, 2026 release gave Olivia Chow a 48% positive personal rating versus 36% for Brad Bradford and showed Olivia Chow leading on affordability, safety, taxes, and working with other governments."

Her apparent lead on *taxes* is notable. At the October 22 debate, Bradford made her record the centre of his closing pitch: he "attacked Olivia Chow's 9.5% and 6.9% tax hikes" and "promised a one-year tax freeze."

**Important caveat on the Chow-favourable signals:** the simulation records that "Pollara conducted polls that were paid for by a union that endorsed Olivia Chow." Two of the strongest pro-Chow data points come from this union-funded source, so the projection discounts them rather than taking them at face value. That discount is one reason the forecast stops at roughly 62% instead of something higher.

**Why an upset remains very much alive**

The ~38% Bradford share reflects real momentum and a coalition that gained ground in the final weeks:

- **Momentum.** In Liaison's tracking, "Brad Bradford's support rose from 39% on September 4–5, 2026, to 49% on October 18–19, 2026." Mainstreet's Quito Maggi noted that Chow "had led wire to wire in Mainstreet's polling before the race tightened in October 2026."
- **Consolidation of the right-of-centre vote.** "Nanos said on October 13, 2026, that Bradford picked up some of Chris Alexander's voters after Chris Alexander quit on October 6, 2026." Alexander had drawn "about 9–10% in polls fielded between August 4 and September 27, 2026."
- **Geographic and demographic strongholds.** Liaison's October 18–19 poll "had Bradford leading among voters in North York, 56% to 42%, and Etobicoke, 63% to 35%," with "the race close in Scarborough," while Chow led downtown. Nanos also "had Bradford ahead among voters aged 35 and over."
- **A large undecided pool.** "Nanos still found 16% undecided in its October 15–18, 2026 poll." That is large enough to overturn any of the late margins.
- **Cross-party endorsements.** "Several federal Liberal MPs backed Bradford by October 5, 2026," and "Lisa Raitt backed Bradford, as listed on October 23, 2026."

The simulated record also contains some inconsistency. One version of the October 18–19 survey puts "Olivia Chow at 49% and Brad Bradford at 48%." Another reports Bradford ahead 49% to 47%. This kind of measurement noise is why the simulation treats the final-week polls as a statistical tie rather than a signal for either side.

**Structural factors: plurality rules and a quick, decisive count**

The rules leave little room for unusual resolution paths. "The mayor in the 2026 Toronto mayoral election is elected by simple plurality with no runoff because Ontario Bill 218 removed the ranked-ballot option." With the race effectively reduced to two candidates since October 6, the plurality rule works like a head-to-head majority contest. Alexander's lingering ballot presence ("too late for Chris Alexander's name to be removed from the ballot") and the 53-name ballot cause only marginal vote leakage.

The outcome is also expected to be known almost immediately. "Unofficial ward-by-ward results covering advance, mail-in, and election-day votes will be posted after polls close on the night of October 26, 2026." In Ontario, "a recount is automatic only in a tie; otherwise it requires a council or board resolution or court order." The simulation therefore assigns negligible probability to a result still being unresolved by the June 30, 2027 deadline, or to an interim appointment. Nearly all of the forecast's uncertainty concerns *who* wins, not *whether* a winner is declared.

**A note on the evidence base:** an attempt to interview simulated agents (pollsters, both campaigns, an independent forecaster and a newspaper) for first-person predictions failed because the simulation environment was no longer running. This section therefore rests on the polling, endorsement and procedural facts recorded in the simulated world, not on agent testimony. In the simulation's own words, both outcomes remain plausible: "The document argues that Olivia Chow could win," and equally, "Brad Bradford could unseat Olivia Chow."

**Bottom line:** the simulated future points to a Chow victory with roughly **62% probability**. Her advantage rests on stronger support among certain and already-voted electors, a higher upper range in the late polls, and better personal ratings. Bradford's late surge, his consolidation of Alexander's former supporters and a 16% undecided bloc keep a Bradford win a genuine possibility.

## How the Agents Behaved: Pollsters, Media, Unions and Campaigns

The simulated race was shaped as much by the institutions around the two candidates as by the candidates themselves. Four kinds of actors drove the narrative in the final weeks: pollsters, news outlets, organized labour and the two campaigns. Each behaved in a recognisable, partisan-coded way. The result was an information environment in which almost every signal could be read as favouring one side or the other.

A methodological note first: we tried to interview simulated agents directly, including campaign staff, Mainstreet Research, The Globe and Mail, CityNews and CUPE Local 416. That attempt failed because the simulation environment was offline. This section therefore relies on the recorded events, statements and relationships in the simulation record, not on first-person testimony.

**Pollsters: a split field that tracks methodology and sponsorship**

The previous section already gave the topline numbers. The behavioural pattern behind them deserves attention. The simulated pollsters did not converge as election day approached. They divided into two camps, and the division lines up closely with how and for whom each poll was conducted.

- **Bradford-leaning results came from automated and text-based polls.** Liaison and Mainstreet both ran IVR polls of 1,000 respondents. Build Canada ran an "SMS-to-online poll of 991 respondents."
- **Chow-leaning results came from Nanos and Pollara.** Nanos ran a phone-and-online poll "for CP24/CTV." Pollara's numbers carry an explicit sponsorship flag in the record:

> "Pollara conducted polls that were paid for by a union that endorsed Olivia Chow."

> "CUPE Local 416 commissioned Pollara to conduct its poll."

This is the clearest case in the simulation of a pollster and a partisan actor working in tandem. The union that endorsed Chow on October 1 later commissioned the poll that produced her widest late lead. Its October 23 release also reported favourable personal and issue ratings. Neither the record nor this report treats the numbers as invalid. Still, a careful reader, and presumably the opposing campaign, could discount them, and the simulation does not show Pollara's figures going unchallenged as a neutral benchmark.

The pollsters also behaved as **narrators of momentum**, not just measurers. Mainstreet's Quito Maggi framed the race as a story of erosion:

> "Mainstreet's polling showed Olivia Chow leading wire to wire before the race tightened in October 2026, according to Mainstreet's Quito Maggi on October 20, 2026."

Nanos gave a structural explanation for Bradford's gains, attributing part of them to the consolidation that plurality rules reward:

> "Nanos said on October 13, 2026, that Bradford picked up some of Chris Alexander's voters after Chris Alexander quit on October 6, 2026."

Liaison's regional breakdowns turned the race into a map. They showed a city split between a Chow downtown and a Bradford suburban belt:

> "Liaison's October 18–19, 2026 poll had Bradford leading among voters in North York, 56% to 42%, and Etobicoke, 63% to 35%."

The same Liaison polling showed "Olivia Chow ahead downtown" and "the race close in Scarborough." This makes Scarborough the swing territory in the simulated contest. Nanos added a demographic layer, finding that its "October 15–18, 2026 poll had Bradford ahead among voters aged 35 and over" while "16% undecided" remained.

The record also carries a reminder of polling's limits. In the 2023 by-election, Mainstreet's final poll underestimated the runner-up:

> "Ana Bailao won 32.46% in the June 26, 2023 by-election, above the 30% in Mainstreet's final poll."

The miss was small. Even so, the presence of this fact in the simulation signals a measure of scepticism toward treating any single firm's final number as decisive. That scepticism is part of why the projection weights the distribution of late polls rather than any one result.

**Media: commissioning, convening and cataloguing**

News outlets in the simulation played three distinct roles.

1. **Commissioning polls that set the frame.** CityNews commissioned a Canada Pulse survey early in the fall that gave the challenger's case its central premise:

> "CityNews commissioned the Canada Pulse poll conducted September 14–24, 2026, which found that 55% said it was time for a new mayor in Toronto."

CP24/CTV sponsored the Nanos tracking that later documented Chow's shrinking lead. Broadcast and local outlets were therefore not passive conduits. They generated the data the campaigns then fought over.

2. **Convening the decisive confrontation.** The final televised debate on October 22 was the last major media-hosted event of the campaign, and Bradford used it to deliver his sharpest contrast (see below).

3. **Cataloguing endorsements.** On October 23, three days before the vote, The Globe and Mail published lists of each candidate's backers. On one side, the record shows the paper listing Kathleen Wynne, "NDP leader Marit Stiles," David Suzuki, Feist and Sarah Polley as endorsers of a female candidate. The text does not name her explicitly, but in a two-way race between Chow and Bradford the reference points to Chow. On the other side, the same October 23 listings record:

> "Shopify CEO Tobias Lutke backed Bradford, as listed on October 23, 2026."

> "Lisa Raitt backed Bradford, as listed on October 23, 2026."

The contrast is telling. Chow's coalition, as the media presented it, drew on provincial NDP and Liberal figures and the cultural and environmental establishment. Bradford's drew on business and former federal Conservatives. The race was thus framed in the final days as a progressive-versus-centre-right choice.

**Unions: endorsement, mobilisation and issue polling**

Organized labour was the most active non-campaign actor on Chow's side, and it worked on two fronts.

**CUPE Local 416** made an early, formal commitment:

> "CUPE Local 416 endorsed Chow on October 1, 2026."

It then extended that commitment into the information battle by commissioning the Pollara surveys. These included the October 23 release that counted "respondents who had already voted." That release reinforced a narrative that Chow's banked advance vote was safe.

**The water workers' union** chose a narrower, issue-driven approach. Instead of simply endorsing, it targeted a specific Bradford policy and used polling as a weapon against it:

> "The water workers' union opposed Brad Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility, as reported on October 21, 2026."

> "The water workers' union commissioned a poll that found 60% were worried about privatization."

This opposition did not come from labour alone. The simulation records that "Fifteen councillors opposed Brad Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility" on the same date. The union campaign turned a technical governance proposal into a privatization fight one day before the final debate. It gave Chow's side a counter-attack against Bradford's fiscal message.

**Campaigns: a tax fight against a governance fight**

The two campaigns pursued opposing strategies, each built on what it saw as the other's weakest flank.

**Bradford's campaign** centred on property taxes and assembled a cross-partisan centre-right coalition. At the October 22 debate:

> "At the October 22, 2026 debate, Bradford attacked Olivia Chow's 9.5% and 6.9% tax hikes."

> "At the October 22, 2026 debate, Bradford promised a one-year tax freeze."

The attack rested on a factual base in the record: "residential property taxes rose 9.5% in 2024 and 6.9% in 2025." The record also notes that council adopted "a smaller 2.2% increase for 2026," which gave Chow a rebuttal. Bradford widened his coalition beyond conservatives early on:

> "Several federal Liberal MPs backed Bradford by October 5, 2026."

That came the day before Alexander withdrew. It positioned Bradford to absorb both centrist Liberal voters and Alexander's former Conservative base. Nanos's later finding that Bradford "picked up some of Chris Alexander's voters" suggests the strategy worked, at least in part. The Lutke and Raitt endorsements reinforced the same coalition in the final days.

**Chow's campaign** relied on incumbency, institutional allies and record-based arguments, not a new offensive. The record notes that "Olivia Chow's 2026 budget froze TTC fares," which gave her a concrete affordability claim to set against the tax attack. The most unusual signal of cross-partisan goodwill came from a figure outside her coalition:

> "Premier Doug Ford publicly praised Olivia Chow on September 16, 2026; Doug Ford's office said the praise was not a formal endorsement."

Ford's office qualified the praise, but it bolstered Chow's claim to "working with other governments." Pollara later found her leading on exactly that dimension. This undercut any attempt by Bradford to tie progressive governance to provincial conflict.

**What the interaction of agents implies**

Taken together, the simulated agents built a feedback loop. Each side's allies produced evidence for its own story:

- Labour-commissioned polls (Pollara, the water workers' survey) supported a Chow narrative of a safe lead, banked advance votes and voter fear of privatization.
- Independent and automated polls (Mainstreet, Liaison, Build Canada), together with Nanos's account of Alexander's voters, supported a Bradford narrative of momentum, suburban consolidation and a city that, per CityNews's Canada Pulse poll, wanted "a new mayor."
- The media amplified both by commissioning polls, hosting the tax-focused debate and publishing rival endorsement lists on October 23.

No actor in the simulation produced a decisive, neutral signal that broke the deadlock. That is consistent with the projection's view of a genuinely contested race. Chow's edge rests on her more motivated, partly banked base and her institutional allies. Bradford's path depends on his late consolidation of the centre-right and suburban vote turning into election-day turnout.

## Key Dynamics Driving the Result: Consolidation, Turnout and Advance Voting

Three structural forces shaped the simulated race after Chris Alexander's exit: **how the anti-incumbent vote consolidated, how many Torontonians turned out, and when they voted.** Each one produced a different winner depending on its weight. Together they explain why the projection favours Chow without being comfortable.

**Consolidation: a centre-right merger that was real but incomplete**

Toronto's rules turned consolidation into the central strategic question. The simulation records that the mayor "is elected by simple plurality with no runoff because Ontario Bill 218 removed the ranked-ballot option." With no second round, any anti-Chow vote that stayed split among challengers helped the incumbent.

Alexander's share was meaningful but had already shrunk before he left. The record shows he "drew about 9–10% in polls fielded between August 4 and September 27, 2026," and had slipped to 4% among decided voters by the Nanos poll of September 30–October 4. His exit then took a form that limited the transfer to Bradford:

> "Alexander ended Alexander's campaign on October 6, 2026, did not endorse anyone, and remained on the ballot."

> "Chris Alexander withdrew from the October 26, 2026 Toronto mayoral election on October 6, 2026, too late for Chris Alexander's name to be removed from the ballot."

Three features of this exit kept the merger leaky:

- **No endorsement.** Alexander's voters received no instruction, so each had to choose a direction alone.
- **His name stayed on the ballot.** On a ballot that "lists 53 names," some residual votes for Alexander, and scattered votes for minor candidates, will not go to Bradford.
- **The timing was awkward.** Alexander quit on October 6, the first day of advance voting. Supporters who voted early may have done so while he was still treated as a live option.

Bradford still captured a real share of the freed-up vote. The graph links the two men directly ("Brad Bradford --[GAINED_VOTERS_FROM]--> Chris Alexander"), and Liaison's tracking measured how far he climbed:

> "Liaison's poll found Brad Bradford's support rose from 39% on September 4–5, 2026, to 49% on October 18–19, 2026."

A ten-point gain is larger than Alexander's peak share. Bradford's consolidation therefore drew on more than Alexander's former voters. It also drew on a wider mood for change that the simulation documents clearly:

> "Build Canada's October 11–16, 2026 poll found that 60% wanted a major change in direction and 61% said Toronto was worse than four years earlier."

> "CityNews commissioned the Canada Pulse poll conducted September 14–24, 2026, which found that 55% said it was time for a new mayor in Toronto."

This is the main threat to Chow in the simulated race. A majority of voters say they want change, and the race has become a straight referendum between her and one alternative.

The endorsement record shows the merger working across party lines on Bradford's side. "Several federal Liberal MPs backed Bradford by October 5, 2026," and later lists added the former Conservative minister Lisa Raitt and Shopify CEO Tobias Lütke. This is a centrist-to-conservative coalition, not a purely partisan one.

Chow's coalition consolidated in turn. The Globe and Mail's October 23 list placed NDP leader Marit Stiles, former Liberal premier Kathleen Wynne, David Suzuki, Sarah Polley and Feist behind her, and CUPE Local 416 had endorsed her on October 1. Premier Doug Ford's September 16 praise, which "Doug Ford's office said … was not a formal endorsement," complicates any simple left-right reading. It probably softened efforts to cast the race as a straight progressive-versus-conservative contest.

**The debate and late issues: Bradford's consolidation message versus Chow's defence**

Bradford's late pitch aimed squarely at the change-seeking majority:

> "At the October 22, 2026 debate, Bradford attacked Olivia Chow's 9.5% and 6.9% tax hikes."

> "At the October 22, 2026 debate, Bradford promised a one-year tax freeze."

Bradford also carried a liability that gave Chow's side a mobilisation issue. His plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility drew organised opposition just before the debate:

> "The water workers' union opposed Brad Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility, as reported on October 21, 2026."

> "Fifteen councillors opposed Brad Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility, as reported on October 21, 2026."

> "The water workers' union commissioned a poll that found 60% were worried about privatization."

In the simulation, this "privatisation" frame is Chow's best counterweight to the "change" frame. It lets her campaign recast Bradford's agenda as a risk and not merely a fresh start. The record contains no polling taken after the October 22 debate other than Pollara's October 23 release, which favoured Chow. The debate's net effect is therefore one of the largest open uncertainties in the simulated race.

**Advance voting: a large early vote banked before the race tightened**

The previous sections noted the record advance turnout. This section examines its timing, which is the key dynamic. Advance voting "ran from October 6 to October 11, 2026," and mail-in ballots "were due by noon on October 14, 2026." Both windows closed before the polls registered the late tightening.

The polls taken during the advance-voting week itself showed Chow clearly ahead:

> "Nanos reported Olivia Chow at 51% to 47% on October 8–10, 2026, among 757 respondents."

> "Liaison reported Olivia Chow at 51% to 46% on October 8–9, 2026."

By contrast, all three of Bradford's leads in late polling came from surveys fielded on October 11 or later. The simulated race had two time zones. Early voters cast ballots in an environment where Chow led by four to five points. Election-day voters decide in an environment that is close to tied.

The size of the early bloc makes this matter. A record 222,550 advance votes equal roughly 40% of the total 2022 turnout (563,124) and about 31% of the 2023 by-election turnout (725,333), both figures recorded in the simulation. Even if 2026 turnout rises well above 2023 levels, early votes will be a substantial share of all ballots cast. They were cast largely before Bradford's surge.

This is the mechanism behind the "already voted" signal discussed earlier. Pollara's figures that include voters who have already cast ballots show a wider Chow lead than its overall field figures. Liaison also counted respondents who had already voted, and the record reports its October 18–19 topline in two ways: once as Bradford 49–47, and once as Chow 49–48. That inconsistency is itself a reminder of how fine the margin is.

**Turnout on election day: the variable that decides whether Bradford's surge counts**

The final dynamic is who shows up on October 26. With "about 2.14 million people … eligible to vote" and past turnouts of well under a million, there is a large pool of potential voters. Small shifts in its composition can swing a race this close.

The simulated data point in a consistent direction. Bradford's late support is concentrated among voters who are less likely to have voted already and less firmly committed:

- Nanos found "Brad Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters" but Chow ahead by 4 among the most certain.
- Nanos's October 15–18 poll "had Bradford ahead among voters aged 35 and over," and Nanos "still found 16% undecided."
- Bradford's geographic strength lies in North York and Etobicoke. Chow leads downtown, and "the race [is] close in Scarborough."

This creates an asymmetric turnout bet. If election day brings a heavy, motivated suburban turnout driven by the change mood, and if most undecided voters break Bradford's way, he can overcome Chow's banked advantage. That is roughly the 38% of simulated outcomes in which he wins. If election-day turnout is ordinary, or if downtown and union-backed get-out-the-vote efforts hold their ground, Chow's early-vote cushion and her lead among certain voters carry her across the line.

The simulation does not contain direct observations of field operations, weather, or ward-level turnout on election day itself. Those factors are not available from the record and remain the main residual uncertainty. Unofficial ward-by-ward results "covering advance, mail-in, and election-day votes will be posted after polls close on the night of October 26, 2026." Comparing the advance and election-day columns in those results will be the clearest test of the timing dynamic described here.

**Net effect**

Consolidation and the change mood are Bradford's engines, and they genuinely closed a ten-point gap. Three things blunted them: Alexander's leaky, unendorsed exit; the timing of the advance vote, which banked a large bloc of ballots while Chow still led; and the concentration of Bradford's late gains among less certain voters. Under plurality rules with no runoff, Bradford needs strong election-day turnout to beat the early count. Chow only needs election day to be roughly ordinary. That asymmetry, more than any single poll, is why the projection settles near 62% for Chow.

## Risks and Uncertainty: Paths to a Bradford Upset

The simulation gives Brad Bradford roughly a **38% chance** of winning. That is not a long shot. Chow's edge rests on readings of the evidence that the record itself leaves open. This section sets out the specific mechanisms by which the projected favourite could lose, and judges how plausible each one looks inside the simulated race.

**Risk 1: The late polls are statistically indistinguishable from a tie**

The most basic threat to the Chow projection is that the late polls cannot separate the two candidates. Several of the late results fall within their own reported margins of error:

> "Liaison reported Brad Bradford at 49% to Olivia Chow's 47% on October 18–19, 2026, among 1,000 respondents, with a 3.1-point margin of error."

> "Nanos reported Olivia Chow at 49.8% to 46.6% on October 15–18, 2026, among 762 respondents, with a 3.6-point margin of error."

The Nanos result is a Chow lead, and the Liaison result is a Bradford lead. Both sit inside the noise band. Chow's projected advantage therefore depends less on what any single neutral poll shows and more on the *shape* of the overall distribution. That shape rests heavily on her largest late leads, which came from the union-commissioned Pollara series discussed in the previous section. If those results are discounted, the remaining independent polls describe a race that is close to a pure coin flip.

The record also contains small internal inconsistencies, which add to the uncertainty. Mainstreet's October 16–18 result appears both as "Brad Bradford at 48.9% to Olivia Chow's 47%" and, in the entity summary, as Bradford 48.9% to Chow 47.5%. A separate fact records that "a poll conducted October 18–19, 2026, put Olivia Chow at 49% and Brad Bradford at 48%." That fact covers the same field dates as the Liaison poll showing Bradford ahead 49–47. Differences of this size do not change the overall picture. They do show that even the "consensus" of late polling is fragile at the one- or two-point level that will likely decide the race.

**Risk 2: The trajectory, not the snapshot, favours Bradford**

Every independent tracking series in the simulation moves the same way. The paired readings make the direction clear:

- **Liaison:** Chow "at 51% to 46% on October 8–9, 2026," then "Brad Bradford at 49% to Olivia Chow's 47% on October 18–19, 2026." That is a seven-point net swing in ten days.
- **Nanos:** Chow "at 51% to 47% on October 8–10, 2026," then 49.8% to 46.6% on October 15–18. Over the longer run, "Chow's lead shrank from about 10 points … to about 3 points."

None of the final polls closed fieldwork after October 21. The October 22 debate therefore falls outside every measurement. At that debate, Bradford pressed his strongest issue:

> "At the October 22, 2026 debate, Bradford attacked Olivia Chow's 9.5% and 6.9% tax hikes."

If the momentum visible through October 21 simply continued for five more days, a three-point Nanos lead could disappear by election day. A debate that landed with tax-sensitive suburban voters would speed that up. The simulation does not record any post-debate poll, so this risk cannot be measured, only identified. It is the most direct path to an upset: **the polls were accurate when taken, but the race kept moving after they stopped.**

**Risk 3: Most of the electorate had not yet voted when Bradford surged**

The earlier sections treated the record 222,550 advance votes as a buffer for Chow. Set against the size of the electorate, that buffer looks thinner. The simulation records that "about 2.14 million people are eligible to vote," and gives recent turnout benchmarks of 563,124 in the 2022 general election and 725,333 in the 2023 by-election.

As an illustrative calculation from these recorded figures: if 2026 turnout matched 2023, advance ballots would make up only about three in ten votes cast. The other roughly seventy percent would come from election-day voting and the mail-in pool, whose ballots were "due by noon on October 14, 2026." The banked advance vote is real, but most ballots were still to be cast when the polls showed the race at its tightest.

The claim that advance voters favour Chow is also weaker than it first appears. The strongest support for it is the union-commissioned Pollara release. Liaison's polling, which *also* counted respondents who had already voted, still found Bradford competitive:

> "Liaison's October 15–21, 2026 polls, including an October 18–19 poll counting respondents who had already voted, showed Brad Bradford ahead in North York and Etobicoke and the race close in Scarborough; in the October 18–19 poll, Brad Bradford had 48%."

So the two pollsters that measured already-voted respondents disagree on who leads. The simulation does not contain a clean, independent breakdown of the advance vote by candidate.

**Risk 4: Undecided voters and soft voters lean toward Bradford's demographics**

A large pool of voters remained uncommitted late in the campaign:

> "Nanos still found 16% undecided in its October 15–18, 2026 poll."

That is far larger than any late lead in the record. The simulation does not report how those undecided voters split. The adjacent signals point the wrong way for Chow:

> "Nanos found Brad Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters in its October 15–18, 2026 poll."

> "Nanos's October 15–18, 2026 poll had Bradford ahead among voters aged 35 and over."

Older voters are generally the most reliable to turn out. Chow's advantage among the "most certain" voters is the best argument that the soft vote will not matter. Bradford's lead among older voters cuts against it, because those voters are disproportionately likely to show up on election day. If the undecided 16% resolve along the lines of the change mood recorded elsewhere, a Bradford win becomes plausible. The record shows "55% said it was time for a new mayor" and "60% wanted a major change in direction."

**Risk 5: Scarborough decides it, and the suburbs are already tilted**

The geographic map in the simulation turns the race into a turnout contest between regions. Bradford's suburban margins are large:

> "Liaison's October 18–19, 2026 poll had Bradford leading among voters in North York, 56% to 42%, and Etobicoke, 63% to 35%."

Chow's base is downtown, and Scarborough is recorded only as "close." The upset path is straightforward: **strong suburban turnout in North York and Etobicoke, combined with even a modest Bradford edge in Scarborough, can outweigh a downtown Chow margin.** That is especially true if younger downtown voters turn out at lower rates than older suburban homeowners. Margins as lopsided as 63–35 in Etobicoke mean that small differences in regional turnout have a large effect on the city-wide total.

**Countervailing risk: Bradford's own vulnerabilities**

The upset paths should not be overstated. The simulation also records late friction for Bradford that could cap his gains:

> "Fifteen councillors opposed Brad Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility, as reported on October 21, 2026."

> "The water workers' union opposed Brad Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility, as reported on October 21, 2026."

This gave Chow a concrete line of attack in the final week. The record does not show whether it moved voters. Chow also keeps the personal-rating and issue-trust advantages described earlier. Bradford needs these upset mechanisms to *stack*: momentum continuing, soft voters breaking his way, and suburban turnout outpacing downtown. Any one of them on its own is probably not enough.

**Tail risk: a margin too close to call**

The resolution criteria add a final, low-probability uncertainty. With several polls within two points, a razor-thin result is possible. The simulation records the rules that would then apply:

> "A legal summary dated April 25, 2023 noted that Ontario's Municipal Elections Act makes a recount automatic only in a tie; otherwise, a council or board resolution or a court order is required."

Unofficial ward-by-ward results "covering advance, mail-in, and election-day votes will be posted after polls close on the night of October 26, 2026." A contested outcome is therefore unlikely to push past the June 30, 2027 resolution deadline. Even so, a sub-one-point margin could bring a period of ambiguity, during which "official City of Toronto information is used." This does not change who is likely to win. It is a reminder that in a race this close, the outcome may come down to a few thousand votes.

**Net assessment**

None of these risks overturns the projection, but together they explain why it stops at about 62% rather than higher. The most plausible upset scenario combines three factors: the polled momentum continues through the debate, the 16% undecided break toward change, and suburban election-day turnout overwhelms Chow's banked advance-vote cushion. All three are consistent with the simulated record. That is why a Bradford victory is a realistic outcome, roughly two chances in five.
