# Toronto 2026 Mayoral Race: Simulated Forecast of Olivia Chow's Chances in a Two-Way, Winner-Take-All Contest

> The simulation points to a razor-thin but modestly Chow-leaning race, with polling split almost evenly (three of six late polls favour Chow by 3–11 points, three favour Bradford by 1–4), so Chow's probability of winning is estimated at roughly 58–62%, driven by incumbency, strong early-vote signals and union and organizational mobilization, and tempered by high polling dispersion and turnout risk.

---

## The Race Structure: A Two-Candidate Plurality Contest Decided on the Margin

The simulation turns the contest into a pure head-to-head. Two candidates are left, one round of voting decides it, and the polls show no stable leader. This section covers the rules and field that set the stakes. Later sections can examine the forces that push the result one way or the other.

**A single round, no runoff, no ranked ballot**

The rules leave no room for a second chance. The simulation records the voting system as follows:

> "The mayor in the 2026 Toronto mayoral election is elected by simple plurality with no runoff because Ontario Bill 218 removed the ranked-ballot option."

Whoever finishes first on October 26 wins, even by a single vote. Second-choice preferences are never counted. About 2.14 million people are eligible to vote, and polls are open from 10 a.m. to 8 p.m. Eastern. Unofficial ward-by-ward results covering advance, mail-in and election-day votes are expected after polls close that night.

**A field reduced to two, on a 53-name ballot**

The race has been a straight Chow-Bradford contest since early October:

> "Since October 6, 2026, Chow and Bradford have been the two candidates in the race."

The ballot is longer than the race. Nominations closed on August 21, leaving 53 names on it. Chris Alexander, a former federal Conservative minister who registered on July 29, drew about 9–10% in polls fielded between August 4 and September 27. He then fell to 4% among decided voters in Nanos's September 30–October 4 poll. The simulation records his exit this way:

> "Alexander ended Alexander's campaign on October 6, 2026, did not endorse anyone, and remained on the ballot."

He withdrew too late to be removed. Under plurality rules, any votes his name still draws are simply lost to both leading candidates. The simulation also shows that his departure did not benefit the two sides equally:

> "Nanos said on October 13, 2026, that Bradford picked up some of Chris Alexander's voters after Chris Alexander quit on October 6, 2026."

**Polling that splits almost evenly**

The simulation does not show a clear front-runner. The six polls reported in the final stretch divide evenly:

> "Olivia Chow and Bradford are competing in the Toronto mayoral election scheduled for October 26, 2026; among six news-reported polls fielded October 11–21, 2026, three showed Olivia Chow ahead by 3 to 11 points and three showed Bradford ahead by 1 to 4 points."

The late polls on each side, with their sample sizes and fieldwork dates, show how close the contest is:

- **Chow ahead:** Pollara (n=1,099, October 13–19) had Chow at 53% to Bradford's 46%. Its October 23 release, counting respondents who had already voted, had Chow at 55% to 44%.
- **Bradford ahead:** Liaison (n=1,000, October 18–19, margin of error 3.1 points) had Bradford at 49% to Chow's 47%. Mainstreet (IVR, n=1,000, October 16–18) had Bradford at 48.9% to Chow's 47%, and Build Canada (SMS-to-online, n=991, October 11–16) had Bradford at 48% to Chow's 44%.
- **Effectively tied:** One October 18–19 poll put the two at 49% and 48%.

Every Bradford lead in these polls is smaller than the margin of error. Chow's smaller leads, such as Nanos's October 15–18 result of 49.8% to 46.6% with a 3.6-point margin of error, are inside the error band as well. Only Pollara shows a gap clearly beyond it.

**A lead that evaporated in four weeks**

The race moved sharply toward Bradford through the campaign:

> "Nanos's poll found Chow's lead shrank from about 10 points on September 30–October 4, 2026, to about 3 points on October 15–18, 2026."

> "Liaison's poll found Brad Bradford's support rose from 39% on September 4–5, 2026, to 49% on October 18–19, 2026."

The simulation also records Quito Maggi of Mainstreet saying on October 20 that Chow had led "wire to wire" in Mainstreet's polling before the race tightened in October. The trend therefore favoured the challenger, even though Chow's final-week position remains competitive.

**Where the margin could be decided**

Several features of the simulated electorate suggest the winning margin will come from specific blocs rather than a broad swing:

- **Voter certainty:** In Nanos's October 15–18 poll, 16% of respondents were still undecided. The poll found Chow ahead by 4 points among the most certain voters and Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain ones. Differences in who actually turns out could therefore matter as much as preference.
- **Geography:** Liaison's October 18–19 poll had Bradford leading in North York, 56% to 42%, and in Etobicoke, 63% to 35%. It had Chow ahead downtown and the race close in Scarborough.
- **Age:** Nanos's October 15–18 poll had Bradford ahead among voters aged 35 and over.
- **Turnout:** A record 222,550 people voted in advance polls from October 6 to 11. For comparison, the 2023 by-election drew 725,333 voters and the October 2022 election drew 563,124.

**What a razor-thin outcome would mean procedurally**

A narrow result would not trigger an automatic recount. The simulation records the recount rule as follows:

> "User states that a legal summary dated April 25, 2023 noted that Ontario's Municipal Elections Act makes a recount automatic only in a tie; otherwise, a council or board resolution or a court order is required."

A margin of a point or two would therefore stand unless someone obtained a council or court decision. Within the scenario's own terms, the winner would be whoever leads in the City of Toronto's results.

**A note on the evidence**

An attempt to interview simulated agents about the two-way, plurality dynamic failed because the simulation environment was no longer running. No first-person agent statements are available for this section. The analysis rests on facts recorded in the simulation's knowledge graph. Some records are also inconsistent. Mainstreet's October 16–18 poll appears with Chow at both 47% and 47.5%. One record attributes the 42.6% decided-voter figure to "Ford" rather than Bradford. These discrepancies do not change the broad picture of a two-candidate race with a very narrow margin.

## Polling Signals: Divergent Late Surveys and the Evidence of a Chow Edge

Late polling in the simulation does not converge. Six surveys fielded in the same ten days point in opposite directions, and the gaps between them come from method, sponsorship, timing and which voters each poll captured. This section looks at why they diverge and how much weight the evidence for a Chow edge can bear. The poll toplines are in the previous section. (The simulated-agent interviews could not run because the environment was unavailable, so this section relies on recorded facts rather than first-person quotes.)

**Sponsorship and method: the two best Chow numbers are not equal**

The strongest Chow readings come from Pollara, and the simulation records who paid for them:

> "CUPE Local 416 commissioned Pollara to conduct its poll."

> "CUPE Local 416 endorsed Chow on October 1, 2026."

> "Pollara conducted polls that were paid for by a union that endorsed Olivia Chow."

This does not make Pollara's numbers wrong. It does mean the widest Chow margin comes from a sponsor with a declared stake. The Bradford-leading polls also differ in mode: Liaison and Mainstreet used IVR, and Build Canada used SMS-to-online. Nanos used phone and online. The simulation holds no direct evidence on which mode better captured Toronto's electorate. It does show that the three Bradford-leading polls and the union-sponsored poll cannot all be right.

**Mainstreet's own track record adds uncertainty**

Mainstreet shows Bradford ahead now, and it also says Chow led throughout until October:

> "Quito Maggi is associated with Mainstreet and said on October 20, 2026 that Olivia Chow had led wire to wire in Mainstreet's polling before the race tightened in October 2026."

The simulation also records a past miss by the same firm:

> "Mainstreet's final poll showed Ana Bailao at 30%, while Ana Bailao won 32.46% in the June 26, 2023 by-election."

That was a 2.46-point underestimate of the runner-up in 2023. It is a small error. But it shows that even a firm's final poll can miss by about the size of the current Bradford leads. These polls therefore tell us little about who is actually ahead.

**Who is being measured: certainty, age and geography**

The sharpest evidence of a Chow edge sits beneath the toplines. Nanos's October 15–18 poll split voters by how firm their choice was:

> "Nanos found Olivia Chow ahead by 4 points among the most certain voters in its October 15–18, 2026 poll."

> "Nanos found Brad Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters in its October 15–18, 2026 poll."

With 16% still undecided, this suggests Chow's support is firmer and Bradford's softer. Whether that matters depends on how the soft vote breaks and who actually turns out. Nanos also found a demographic tilt against her:

> "Nanos's October 15–18, 2026 poll had Bradford ahead among voters aged 35 and over."

Liaison's regional breakdown shows a city divided by geography:

> "Liaison's October 18–19, 2026 poll had Bradford leading among voters in North York, 56% to 42%, and Etobicoke, 63% to 35%."

Chow led downtown and the race was close in Scarborough. If those patterns hold, Chow's route to a plurality runs through the downtown core and a competitive Scarborough. Bradford's strength in North York and Etobicoke is large enough to make the citywide margin hinge on relative turnout in these areas.

**The trend line cuts against the incumbent, but unevenly**

Several pollsters show the slide. Liaison had Chow at 51% to 46% on October 8–9. Nanos had her at 51% to 47% on October 8–10. Each firm's next reading shows her lead shrinking or gone: Nanos's lead fell from about ten points to about three, and Liaison's flipped to a two-point Bradford edge. Yet Pollara's Chow lead widened between its mid-October poll and its October 23 release. The simulation does not say whether that reflects real movement or its sponsor's sample.

Pollara's October 23 release also carries non-horserace data favourable to Chow:

> "Pollara's October 23, 2026 release gave Olivia Chow a 48% positive personal rating versus 36% for Brad Bradford and showed Olivia Chow leading on affordability, safety, taxes, and working with other governments."

A personal-rating gap of 12 points on a union-commissioned poll should be read with the same caution as its topline. It is still the only direct measure of candidate favourability in the simulation.

**Early-vote evidence: large, but not decisive**

The simulation records a very large advance vote:

> "A record 222,550 people voted in advance polls for the October 26, 2026 Toronto mayoral election from October 6 to October 11, 2026."

It does not say how those ballots broke. The only way to estimate this comes from polls that counted respondents who had already voted, and they disagree. Pollara's version put Chow at 55% to 44%. Liaison's October 18–19 poll also counted early voters, yet still had Bradford ahead 49% to 47%. So the early vote is not a uniform Chow signal. It strengthens her case only if one trusts the Pollara sample, and it does not close the gap in Liaison's.

**Late-campaign events in the simulation that could affect the polls**

Several recorded developments fall after most fieldwork ended:

- At the October 22 debate, Bradford "promised a one-year tax freeze" and "attacked Olivia Chow's 9.5% and 6.9% tax hikes." The simulation notes that council adopted a smaller 2.2% increase for 2026.
- On October 21, fifteen councillors and the water workers' union opposed Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility.
- Several federal Liberal MPs and Lisa Raitt backed Bradford, while Premier Ford praised Chow without a formal endorsement.

These could move votes, but the simulation records no poll fielded after the debate except Pollara's October 23 release. That release is the only post-debate poll, and it favours Chow.

**What this means for Chow's chances**

The polling evidence supports a modest, fragile Chow lean rather than a clear lead:

- **For Chow:** her support is firmer among the most certain voters, she is ahead downtown, her personal ratings are stronger, and the only post-debate poll favours her.
- **Against Chow:** three of six late polls show Bradford ahead, and in all three his lead is inside the margin of error. He leads in North York and Etobicoke, among voters aged 35 and over, and among less certain voters. Her lead shrank in Nanos's series. Her best numbers come from a poll commissioned by a union that endorsed her.

Weighing these signals alongside the incumbency and mobilization factors covered elsewhere in this report, the simulation's polling picture is consistent with a Chow win probability in the high-50s to low-60s. The dispersion between pollsters is wide enough that a Bradford win would not be surprising.

## Agent Behaviours: Campaigns, Labour, Media and Voters in the Final Stretch

The simulation's interview environment was offline, so no agent could be questioned directly (0 of 36 interviews completed). The behaviours below come from recorded actions, endorsements and reported facts, not first-person statements.

**The closing argument: tax record against tax freeze**

The two campaigns spent the last televised debate, on October 22, on one question: whether Toronto can afford another term of Chow. Bradford went after her property tax record and offered a concrete alternative:

> "At the October 22, 2026 debate, Bradford attacked Olivia Chow's 9.5% and 6.9% tax hikes."

> "At the October 22, 2026 debate, Bradford promised a one-year tax freeze."

The record shows Chow's side had a counter-argument. The simulation notes that her tax increases came early in her term and then eased:

> "Under Olivia Chow, residential property taxes rose 9.5% in 2024 and 6.9% in 2025, and Toronto City Council adopted a smaller 2.2% increase for 2026 on February 10, 2026."

> "Olivia Chow's 2026 budget froze TTC fares."

The incumbent defended a record in which the large increases were followed by a smaller one and a transit fare freeze. The challenger tried to make the earlier increases the main fact about her mayoralty. Pollara's October 23 release suggests the voters it sampled were not fully persuaded by Bradford's framing:

> "Pollara’s October 23, 2026 release gave Olivia Chow a 48% positive personal rating versus 36% for Brad Bradford and showed Olivia Chow leading on affordability, safety, taxes, and working with other governments."

This is the union-commissioned poll, so it should be read with the sponsorship caveat from the previous section. Within the simulation, it is the only evidence that Chow led on taxes and affordability.

**Labour: endorsement, polling and a targeted attack on Bradford's water plan**

Labour's role in the simulation goes beyond endorsing. CUPE Local 416 endorsed Chow, then paid for polling that put her ahead:

> "CUPE Local 416 endorsed Chow on October 1, 2026."

The water workers' union opened a separate front against Bradford on a single policy, and the opposition spread beyond organized labour:

> "The water workers' union opposed Brad Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility, as reported on October 21, 2026."

> "Fifteen councillors opposed Brad Bradford's plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility, as reported on October 21, 2026."

The union also backed the attack with its own research. A poll it commissioned found that "60% were worried about privatization". The simulation shows labour using the final week to turn a technical governance proposal into a privatization story. It does not show whether that story reached beyond voters already inclined toward Chow.

**Endorsers: a Bradford coalition from the centre-right, and a restrained Ford**

Bradford's endorsements came from business, federal Liberal and Conservative-leaning figures:

- "Several federal Liberal MPs backed Bradford by October 5, 2026."
- "Lisa Raitt backed Bradford, as listed on October 23, 2026."
- "Shopify CEO Tobias Lutke backed Bradford, as listed on October 23, 2026."

Premier Doug Ford, who might have been expected to help the challenger, stayed outside the contest. The record shows him praising Chow:

> "Premier Doug Ford publicly praised Olivia Chow on September 16, 2026; Doug Ford’s office said the praise was not a formal endorsement."

The Globe and Mail's October 23 list shows a separate group of cultural and political figures. It names Kathleen Wynne, David Suzuki, NDP leader Marit Stiles, Feist and Sarah Polley as endorsers of "the unnamed woman". The simulation does not name the candidate in these records. Chow is the only woman among the two leading candidates, so the endorsements most plausibly belong to her, but the record does not confirm it. If they are Chow's, the pattern is clear: Chow's backers are labour, progressive and cultural names, and Bradford's are business and centrist-to-conservative ones. Neither list is recorded as shifting the polls.

**Voters: heavy early participation and a challenger-leaning shift among Alexander's former supporters**

Voters were already acting before the final weekend. Advance voting was heavy:

> "A record 222,550 people voted in advance polls for the October 26, 2026 Toronto mayoral election from October 6 to October 11, 2026."

The simulation does not break these votes down by candidate or ward. Two late polls were at least partly measuring them, and they point in opposite directions:

- Pollara's October 23 release: "Chow at 55% to Bradford's 44%, counting respondents who had already voted."
- Liaison's October 18–19 poll, which also counted respondents who had already voted, had Bradford at 48–49% and Chow at 47–49% depending on the reported version, with Bradford strong in North York and Etobicoke.

The record also gives turnout comparisons: 563,124 votes in the October 24, 2022 election and 725,333 in the June 26, 2023 by-election. Both come from a user-supplied statement. The simulation leaves open how the 2026 vote will compare, but a turnout well above the 2022 figure would be consistent with the early-vote signal.

Alexander's former supporters were also moving. Nanos reported that "Bradford picked up some of Chris Alexander's voters after Chris Alexander quit on October 6, 2026." Alexander stayed on the ballot without endorsing anyone, so this was a voter-level drift toward the challenger, not a campaign-directed transfer.

**What the agent behaviour implies**

The simulation shows three campaign dynamics going into October 26:

1. **Bradford was fighting to define the race around taxes**, using the debate and a one-year freeze pledge.
2. **Chow's side relied on organized support:** union endorsement and polling, the water-privatization attack, and a record of eased tax increases and frozen TTC fares.
3. **Voters were splitting by method, geography and timing.** Early voters, Alexander's former supporters and the North York–Etobicoke versus downtown divide all pulled in different directions.

The simulation does not record any turnout operation, door-knocking volume or voter-contact figures for either campaign. Its evidence of mobilization is limited to endorsements, union polling and the advance-vote total, so any claim about relative ground-game strength goes beyond what it contains.

## Probability Assessment and Key Risks: Why Chow Is a Modest Favourite, Not a Lock

**The probability estimate: about 60% for Chow**

The simulation's evidence supports a Chow win probability of roughly 58–62%, which I put at about 60%. That makes her a modest favourite. The record does not support anything stronger, because the central fact in it is a polling field that divides evenly:

> "Olivia Chow and Bradford are competing in the Toronto mayoral election scheduled for October 26, 2026; among six news-reported polls fielded October 11–21, 2026, three showed Olivia Chow ahead by 3 to 11 points and three showed Bradford ahead by 1 to 4 points."

Under a simple plurality rule with no runoff, a 60/40 estimate means Bradford wins about two times in five. That fits a race in which most readings sit inside the margin of error.

**Why the estimate leans toward Chow**

Four pieces of evidence tilt the balance, and none is decisive alone.

- **Firmer support among the most certain voters.** Nanos's October 15–18 poll found Chow's support was the more committed:

> "Nanos found Olivia Chow ahead by 4 points among the most certain voters in its October 15–18, 2026 poll."

- **Votes already cast.** Pollara's October 23 release, which counted respondents who had already voted, had Chow at 55% to Bradford's 44%. The record also says a record 222,550 people voted in advance polls from October 6 to 11. By simple arithmetic, that is roughly 10% of the 2.14 million eligible voters. Chow's lead in the poll that counts early voters comes from a union-commissioned sample, so it carries the sponsorship caveat from the polling section. The simulation does not record how the advance ballots actually split.
- **Favourable personal ratings and issue leads.** The same Pollara release gave Chow a 48% positive personal rating against 36% for Bradford, and showed her ahead on affordability, safety, taxes and working with other governments. This is the only evidence in the record that she wins the tax argument Bradford made central at the October 22 debate.
- **A track record of winning.** Chow won the June 26, 2023 by-election with 37.16%, ahead of Ana Bailao at 32.46%, and Mainstreet's Quito Maggi said she led "wire to wire" in his firm's polling until October. Doug Ford also praised her on September 16, although his office said this was not a formal endorsement.

**Why she is not a lock**

Three things cut against her, and they add up to a real chance of defeat.

- **Momentum is with the challenger.** Nanos found her lead shrank "from about 10 points on September 30–October 4, 2026, to about 3 points on October 15–18, 2026." Liaison's Bradford figure rose from 39% to 49% between early September and October 18–19. Mainstreet, Liaison and Build Canada all had Bradford ahead in their latest readings.
- **Appetite for change.** Build Canada found that 60% wanted a major change in direction and 61% thought Toronto was worse than four years earlier. A Canada Pulse poll for CityNews in September found that 55% said it was time for a new mayor. Those are sentiment measures, not vote-intention measures, but they are the conditions in which incumbents lose.
- **Late-deciding voters lean Bradford.** Nanos recorded 16% undecided and found Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters. It also found him ahead among those aged 35 and over. Nanos said that Bradford "picked up some of Chris Alexander's voters after Chris Alexander quit on October 6, 2026." Alexander's name stays on the ballot, so some of his remaining supporters may still vote for him and help neither candidate.

**Turnout and geography: the main swing factor**

Geography makes turnout the largest uncertainty. Liaison had Bradford leading North York 56% to 42% and Etobicoke 63% to 35%, while Chow led downtown and Scarborough was close. If turnout is stronger in the suburban wards than in the core, a Bradford plurality becomes plausible even if Chow wins the downtown vote by a wide margin.

The record offers turnout baselines that show how much the electorate has swung in recent contests:

> "User states that turnout in the October 24, 2022 election was 563,124."

> "User states that turnout in the June 26, 2023 by-election was 725,333."

Turnout therefore rose by roughly 160,000 voters between those two contests. The simulation holds no forecast of 2026 turnout, so it cannot say whether a surge helps one candidate. The record-setting advance vote only shows that engagement was high.

**Mode and sponsorship uncertainty**

The sponsorship and mode differences covered earlier mean the Pollara lead cannot simply be averaged with the three Bradford-leading polls. The record also gives two readings for the October 18–19 Liaison poll: Bradford 49% to Chow's 47%, and Chow 49% to Bradford 48%. That shows how little separates the candidates and how sensitive the picture is to which reading you take.

**A close result and the resolution rules**

A narrow margin carries its own risk of a delayed outcome. The simulation records that in Ontario "a recount is automatic only in a tie; otherwise, a council or board resolution or a court order is required." A very narrow result could therefore stay unsettled for some time. The question resolves by "a consensus of credible reporting," with official City of Toronto information used if reporting is ambiguous. Unofficial ward-by-ward results covering advance, mail-in and election-day votes are due after polls close on October 26.

**What would change the estimate**

- **Toward Chow:** a strong downtown and Scarborough turnout, advance-vote tallies that match the Pollara pattern, and the firmer decided vote holding as late deciders split.
- **Toward Bradford:** heavy turnout in North York and Etobicoke, late deciders breaking toward him as in Nanos's less certain group, and a larger share of former Alexander supporters than the record shows going to him.

The simulation points to a Chow win in about three of five outcomes. The evidence for her is firmer support and early votes. The evidence against her is the trend and the three polls that now favour Bradford.
