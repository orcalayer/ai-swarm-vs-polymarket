# Toronto 2026 Mayoral Election Forecast: Will Olivia Chow Win?

> The simulated future points to a narrow but leaning Chow victory: polling shows Chow ahead in most recent surveys, yet the race remains close enough that Bradford could win on a late shift.

---

## Baseline Trajectory: Chow's Polling Lead and Its Durability

The simulated polling record for the Toronto mayoral race shows a lead that was large in early autumn, narrowed steadily through October, and by the final week had become a near-tie in which the result could go either way. Chow's position is strongest in the aggregate of firms that polled earlier and in the late Pollara release, while the most recent independent surveys show Bradford at or slightly ahead of her.

**Early-September strength**

The race opened with a clear Chow advantage. Liaison Strategies' IVR poll, fielded September 4–5, 2026 with 1,000 respondents, put her at 50% against 39% for Brad Bradford. Nanos Research's phone and online poll for CP24/CTV, fielded September 30–October 4 with 755 respondents, reported decided-voter support of 52.4% for Chow, 42.6% for Bradford and 4% for Chris Alexander. Nanos summarized the trend across its own series: Chow's lead "shrank from about 10 points on September 30–October 4, 2026, to about 3 points on October 15–18, 2026."

**Mid-October compression**

The October 8–10 and October 8–9 readings still favoured Chow, with Nanos showing her at 51% to 47% and Liaison at 51% to 46%. From the middle of the month, however, the spread narrowed quickly. Build Canada's SMS-to-online poll of 991 respondents, fielded October 11–16, had Bradford narrowly ahead, at 48% to Chow's 44%. Mainstreet Research's IVR poll of 1,000 respondents, fielded October 16–18, put Bradford at 48.9% and Chow at 47%. Nanos's October 15–18 poll, with 762 respondents and a 3.6-point margin of error, had Chow at 49.8% to 46.6%, with 16% of respondents undecided.

The pattern is one of convergence rather than a single collapse. Liaison's Bradford figure rose from 39% in early September to 49% in its October 18–19 poll, which recorded Bradford at 49% and Chow at 47% with a 3.1-point margin of error.

**Late-October readings and durability**

Late polling did not settle on one story. A poll conducted October 18–19 put Chow at 49% and Bradford at 48%. Pollara's poll of 1,099 respondents, fielded October 13–19, measured Chow at 53% to 46%, and its October 23 release reported Chow at 55% to 44%, counting respondents who had already voted. Among six news-reported polls fielded October 11–21, three showed Chow ahead by 3 to 11 points and three showed Bradford ahead by 1 to 4 points.

The durability of Chow's lead depends heavily on which voters are counted. Nanos found Chow ahead by 4 points among the most certain voters, but Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters in the same October 15–18 poll. Liaison's October 18–19 poll showed Bradford leading in North York (56% to 42%) and Etobicoke (63% to 35%), while Chow was ahead downtown. Nanos also reported that Bradford had picked up some of Chris Alexander's voters after Alexander withdrew on October 6, a shift that the record links directly to Bradford's rising numbers.

Mainstreet's Quito Maggi said on October 20 that Chow "had led wire to wire" in the firm's polling before the race tightened, and a local tracking document states that her lead "narrowed sharply in October 2026." Taken together, the simulated record describes a contest that is still leaning toward Chow on the strongest recent polls but has lost much of its margin, with Bradford positioned to win on a late shift among the less certain and suburban voters who appear most movable in the final days.

## Agent Behaviour: Voter Blocs, Pollsters, Media and Labour Reactions

The simulated record for this section is thinner than the polling chapter. Interviews with individual agents failed because the simulation environment was not running, so there are no first-person quotes from voter blocs, journalists or union members. What the simulation does show comes from endorsements, union-commissioned polling, the candidates' policy fights and a handful of media-reported facts.

**Labour: a clear split, and a union that moved against Bradford**

Labour was the most visibly engaged organisation in the simulated race. CUPE Local 416 endorsed Chow on October 1, 2026, and commissioned Pollara to conduct its poll, which measured Chow at 53% to Bradford's 46% in fieldwork from October 13–19. The water workers' union then became the sharpest critic of Bradford's platform. The simulated record reports that the union opposed his plan to turn Toronto Water into a utility, and that a poll it commissioned found 60% of respondents worried about privatization. Fifteen councillors also opposed the utility plan, as reported on October 21, 2026.

Because the Pollara poll was paid for by a union that had endorsed Chow, its figures carry an obvious partisan origin. The simulation records this fact alongside the numbers rather than treating the poll as neutral.

**Voter blocs: the policy fight over taxes and water**

The bloc-level behaviour that emerges centres on two issues. Bradford's main line of attack was on Chow's record. At the October 22 debate, the last televised meeting between the two, he attacked her 9.5% and 6.9% tax hikes and promised a one-year tax freeze. The record notes that Chow's 2026 increase was smaller, at 2.2%, adopted on February 10, 2026, and that her 2026 budget froze TTC fares. Pollara's October 23 release gave Chow a 48% positive personal rating against 36% for Bradford, with Chow leading on affordability, safety, taxes and working with other governments.

Bradford's support was strongest in the suburbs. Liaison's October 18–19 poll had him ahead in North York (56% to 42%) and Etobicoke (63% to 35%), while Chow led downtown. Nanos found Chow ahead by 4 points among the most certain voters, but Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters, and reported that Bradford had picked up some of Chris Alexander's voters after Alexander quit on October 6. Alexander's exit left his name on the ballot, and the simulated record does not say where his remaining supporters went.

**Media and pollsters: a contested picture**

The simulated media record is mostly a record of poll releases rather than editorial positions. Mainstreet's Quito Maggi said on October 20, 2026 that Chow "had led wire to wire" before the race tightened, and a local tracking document states her lead "narrowed sharply in October 2026." Among six news-reported polls fielded October 11–21, three showed Chow ahead by 3 to 11 points and three showed Bradford ahead by 1 to 4 points. The simulation does not record a clear editorial verdict from The Globe and Mail or CityNews, so this section cannot describe any media endorsement.

The pollsters themselves disagreed on the same race. Liaison's Bradford figure rose from 39% in early September to 49% in its October 18–19 poll, while Pollara's late release had Chow at 55%. The gap between firms is therefore as much a feature of agent behaviour as the voters themselves.

**Turnout signals**

Advance voting was heavy: a record 222,550 people voted in advance polls from October 6 to 11, 2026. Among the 2.14 million eligible voters, that early turnout is a sign of an engaged electorate, though the simulation does not link it to either candidate's bloc.

**Summary of the behavioural picture**

The simulated agents show a left-leaning labour movement and a downtown electorate behind Chow, and a suburban and less-certain electorate that moved toward Bradford through October. Neither the media nor the unions produced a decisive turn in the final week that the simulation records. The outcome in this section therefore depends on whether the suburban and low-certainty voters who drove Bradford's late gains turn out in comparable numbers to the downtown and labour-backed vote that carried Chow's earlier lead.

## Emergent Risks: Polling Uncertainty and Plurality Dynamics

The simulated record points to a risk that is less about a single poll error and more about how a plurality rule, a crowded ballot and a late-moving electorate interact. Chow enters the final days with the stronger aggregate position, but the margin is thin enough that the outcome could turn on which voters actually show up.

**Undecided voters and the thinness of the lead**

The clearest signal of uncertainty comes from Nanos Research's October 15–18 poll, which recorded 16% of respondents as undecided. That poll put Chow at 49.8% to 46.6% with a 3.6-point margin of error, meaning the reported gap sits inside the statistical noise. The same series shows the broader erosion: Nanos reported that Chow's lead "shrank from about 10 points on September 30–October 4, 2026, to about 3 points on October 15–18, 2026." A lead of that size, combined with a sizeable undecided share, leaves little room for error in either direction.

The simulated record also suggests that undecided and less committed voters are not evenly distributed. Nanos found Chow ahead by 4 points among the most certain voters, but Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters in the same poll. Nanos's October 15–18 poll also had Bradford ahead among voters aged 35 and over. If the late-deciding group is the one leaning toward Bradford, the headline lead overstates Chow's firmness.

**Poll disagreement as a source of uncertainty**

The six news-reported polls fielded October 11–21 split evenly in direction: "three showed Olivia Chow ahead by 3 to 11 points and three showed Bradford ahead by 1 to 4 points." Mainstreet's IVR poll, fielded October 16–18, put Bradford at 48.9% to Chow's 47%, while Liaison's October 18–19 poll had Bradford at 49% to Chow's 47% with a 3.1-point margin of error. Pollara's October 23 release, which counted respondents who had already voted, gave Chow 55% to 44%. The gap between these readings is larger than any single margin of error, which suggests that the sampling and likely-voter assumptions used by each firm are carrying much of the disagreement.

Mainstreet's Quito Maggi said on October 20, 2026 that Chow "had led wire to wire" in the firm's polling before the race tightened, a statement that describes the trend but does not settle which final reading is closest to the vote.

**Plurality and the vote split on the ballot**

Because Ontario Bill 218 removed the ranked-ballot option, the mayor is "elected by simple plurality with no runoff." That rule matters for the Chow-Bradford contest in two ways. First, it means the winner needs only the most votes, so a narrow lead among a fragmented field is enough. Second, the ballot is crowded: the October 26 ballot lists 53 names, and Chris Alexander's name remained on it after he quit on October 6 without endorsing anyone. Nanos reported that Bradford picked up some of Alexander's voters after the withdrawal, which is the one documented case in the record of vote movement between the two leading candidates. The simulated record does not say where Alexander's remaining supporters went, so the effect of the split on the final count cannot be measured from the material available.

**Turnout as the swing variable**

Turnout is the factor the record treats as most consequential for the plurality outcome. A record 222,550 people voted in advance polls from October 6 to 11, out of about 2.14 million eligible voters. Heavy early participation points to an engaged electorate, but the simulation does not tie early voters to either candidate. Liaison's poll counting respondents who had already voted gave Chow a clearer advantage than polls of all respondents, which indicates that the composition of the voting population, rather than the headline number, is where the outcome is likely to be decided.

**Scenarios that favour each candidate**

The simulated record supports two broad paths to the result:

- **A Chow win** would most likely come if her downtown base and the early-voting population turned out in larger numbers than the less certain suburban voters, consistent with Pollara's and Nanos's most-certain-voter readings.
- **A Bradford win** would most likely come if the less certain voters, the North York and Etobicoke electorate where Liaison found him ahead by 14 and 28 points, and voters who moved over from Alexander's campaign broke his way in the final days.

On the balance of the simulated record, Chow remains the more likely plurality winner, but the combination of a 3.6-point margin of error, a 16% undecided share, an evenly split poll record and a crowded ballot means the simulation does not support a comfortable lead. The outcome is best read as a close race in which the plurality rule rewards whichever side mobilises its voters most effectively in the last days.

## Forecast Conclusion: Probability Estimate for Chow Winning

The simulated record supports a narrow Chow lead in the aggregate, with a real chance that Bradford wins on a late shift. Weighing the evidence, this report puts the probability that Olivia Chow wins the October 26, 2026 election at **56%**.

**Evidence favouring Chow**

Chow's strongest support comes from the most recent poll that counted early voters and from the firms that polled her ahead for longest. Pollara's October 23 release reported "Chow at 55% to Bradford's 44%, counting respondents who had already voted." Pollara's broader October 13–19 fieldwork had her at 53% to 46%, though that poll was commissioned by CUPE Local 416, which had endorsed her on October 1.

Chow also has a record that the simulation treats as an asset. She "won the June 26, 2023 Toronto mayoral by-election with 37.16% of the vote," and the record notes that Pollara's release gave her a 48% positive personal rating against 36% for Bradford. Nanos's October 15–18 poll found "Chow ahead by 4 points among the most certain voters," which is the group most likely to turn out.

**Evidence favouring Bradford**

The counterweight is the trend and the composition of the late-deciding vote. Nanos's lead for Chow "shrank from about 10 points on September 30–October 4, 2026, to about 3 points on October 15–18, 2026," and in that same poll "Bradford ahead by 4 points among less certain voters." Liaison's October 18–19 poll had Bradford at 49% to Chow's 47%, and Mainstreet's October 16–18 IVR poll had him at 48.9% to 47%. Build Canada's October 11–16 poll also had Bradford narrowly ahead.

The simulated record also shows Bradford gaining ground in the suburbs and picking up support after Chris Alexander's exit: Liaison had him ahead in "North York, 56% to 42%, and Etobicoke, 63% to 35%." Build Canada found "60% wanted a major change in direction," which is a signal that favours the challenger.

**How the plurality rule and the field affect the estimate**

Because the mayor is elected "by simple plurality with no runoff," a narrow lead is enough for either candidate. The ballot lists 53 names, and Alexander's name remained on it, so some of his former supporters may still vote for him rather than move to either leader. This reduces the weight of any single lead.

Undecided voters add further uncertainty. Nanos still found "16% undecided" in its October 15–18 poll, and the simulated record does not indicate how that group will split. Given that the six news-reported polls fielded October 11–21 divided evenly ("three showed Olivia Chow ahead... and three showed Bradford ahead"), the averages give Chow a modest edge rather than a decisive one.

**Why the estimate sits at 56%**

- Chow leads the most recent poll that counted early voters, and her lead holds among the most certain voters.
- Bradford has the momentum in the trend lines and the strongest signal among less certain and suburban voters.
- The independent polls from mid-to-late October are split, so no single reading should dominate the estimate.
- The election is decided by turnout, which the simulation does not link to either candidate beyond the record-breaking 222,550 advance votes.

The simulated future therefore points to a Chow victory as more likely than not, but only just. The outcome would be most sensitive to whether the less certain, suburban voters who favoured Bradford in late October actually turned out in larger numbers than the more certain, downtown voters who favoured Chow.
