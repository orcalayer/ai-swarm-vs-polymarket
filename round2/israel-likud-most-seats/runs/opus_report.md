# Pre-Election Forecast for the 26th Knesset: Likud Trails Yashar Narrowly in the Final Mainstream Polls, Putting Its Bid for Largest List at Roughly 38%

> In the simulated final week before the 27 October 2026 election, most broadcast and newspaper polls show Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar one or two seats ahead of Likud, with Likud second at 19–21 seats. Only Direct Polls dissents, giving Likud a 6-seat lead. Because the gap is within normal polling error, the simulation estimates about a 38% chance that Likud wins the most seats.

---

## The Final Pre-Blackout Poll Picture: A Narrow Yashar Edge and One Outlier

In the last days before the legal blackout, the simulation produces a clear but fragile picture. Every mainstream broadcast and newspaper poll places Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar first and Likud second, by margins of two to four seats. The only polls showing Likud ahead come from two outlets with a known pro-Likud lean. Because no new polls may be published until the 22:00 exit polls on 27 October, these numbers are the last public evidence voters, campaigns and forecasters will have.

**The final mainstream readings: Yashar ahead by 2 to 4 seats**

On 23 October, the last permitted publication day, the three main TV channels all published polls with the same order. The simulated record sums them up:

> "In the three final TV polls of 23 October 2026, Likud trailed Yashar by 2 to 3 seats."

| Outlet (pollster) | Date published | Yashar | Likud | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Channel 12 | 23 Oct | 23 | 21 | Yashar +2 |
| Channel 13 | 23 Oct | 22 | 19 | Yashar +3 |
| Kan 11 | 23 Oct | 22 | 20 | Yashar +2 |
| Maariv (Lazar Research, fieldwork 19–22 Oct) | 23 Oct | 21 | 19 | Yashar +2 |
| Israel Hayom (Kantar) | 22 Oct | 23 | 21 | Yashar +2 |
| Walla | 20 Oct | 23 | 19 | Yashar +4 |

The Kan figure has a small inconsistency. The record notes that Kan "reported a poll projecting 22 seats for Yashar and 20 seats for Likud; the poll information also mentions Likud at 19 seats without a clearly identified source." Either way, Kan's order does not change.

Maariv was the only final poll with a full three-party line. Naftali Bennett's Together was third:

> "A Maariv poll by Lazar Research, published on 23 October 2026 after fieldwork from 19 to 22 October 2026, projected 21 seats for Yashar, 19 for Likud, and 15 for Together."

Across the four polls published on the final day, Yashar averages about 22 seats and Likud just under 20. Likud's position in these polls is consistent: always second, never more than three seats behind on the last day, and never ahead.

**The outliers: Direct Polls and Channel 14**

The polls that disagree come from two sources. The one the report focuses on is Direct Polls, which polls for i24NEWS:

> "Direct Polls conducted a poll for i24NEWS on 20 October 2026 that projected Likud at 26 seats and Yashar at 20 seats."

Channel 14 goes further. Its 22 October poll projects "30 seats for Likud and 20 seats for Yashar." According to the simulated record, Shlomo Filber runs Channel 14's polls. Jewish Insider labels both outlets' polls outliers: it "called Channel 14's polls outliers" and "called i24NEWS polls outliers."

The clearest way to see the split is through the whole late-campaign dataset:

> "Among 41 published polls with fieldwork from 27 September to 22 October 2026, Likud led 14 polls, all published by Channel 14 or i24NEWS."

> "Among 41 published polls with fieldwork from 27 September to 22 October 2026, Yashar led 25 polls."

The remaining two of the 41 were ties. So Likud's lead appears only in polls from these two outlets and in none of the others. That pattern looks like a consistent pollster bias rather than real disagreement about the race.

**Why the outlier cannot be dismissed outright**

The simulation gives Direct Polls some credibility from the last election. On 22 October Jewish Insider noted that Direct Polls "was regarded as the most accurate pollster of the previous election." The record also shows the opposite risk:

> "Direct Polls gave Likud 34 seats in its poll of 27 October 2022, overshooting the 32 seats Likud won on 1 November 2022."

> "Mainstream pollsters underestimated Likud in the 2022 election: their final pre-election polls from 26–28 October 2022 gave Likud 30 or 31 seats, while Likud won 32 seats on 1 November 2022."

In 2022, then, the mainstream polls were one or two seats too low on Likud, and Direct Polls was two seats too high. If both errors repeat in 2026, the two pictures converge toward a near-tie between Likud and Yashar. A mainstream miss of one to two seats is roughly the same size as Yashar's final two-to-three-seat lead. This is why the simulation treats Likud's chances as real but below even.

**Direction of travel: Likud recovered, then stalled**

The trend across the campaign shows both a Likud rally and a late Yashar lead. Polls in late September and early October showed a dead heat:

> "Zman Yisrael reported Likud and Yashar tied at 21 seats in a poll conducted on 30 September–1 October 2026."

> "Channel 13 reported Likud and Yashar tied at 20 seats on 7 October 2026."

Measured over the full campaign, Likud's position improved from a low point:

> "According to the JC average of 22 October 2026, Likud had recovered from about 17 seats in September 2026 to about 20 seats."

By the final week, though, Yashar had moved back to a two-to-three-seat lead in the mainstream polls. Likud regained ground after its September low but has not, in any mainstream poll, overtaken Yashar.

**The soft-support signal**

The strongest point in Likud's favor is how committed its voters are, not its seat count:

> "A Walla poll published on 20 October 2026 found that 68% of Likud voters were completely sure of their vote, compared with 40% of Yashar voters and 23% of Together voters."

That gap in certainty matters, and the record points out that much of Yashar's soft support could move to its own allies rather than to Likud:

> "Some of Yashar's soft support could still drift to its own allies; Together gained two seats in the Maariv poll of 23 October 2026."

If some of Yashar's uncommitted voters switch to Together, Yashar's lead shrinks without Likud gaining a seat. That is one plausible way Likud could finish first as the largest list while still polling around 19–21.

Likud also faces leakage on its right. Haaretz reported on 23 October that "56% of religious Zionists who voted Likud in 2022 did not plan to vote Likud this time and were leaning toward Itamar Ben-Gvir's or Bezalel Smotrich's parties." This works against the turnout advantage suggested by Likud's high voter certainty.

**Bloc numbers are a distraction for this question**

The final TV polls also showed neither bloc reaching 61 seats, with "three TV polls on 23 October 2026" giving the opposition bloc 52 to 56 seats. Under the forecast's resolution rules, bloc totals do not count. The only question is whether Likud's own list finishes with the most seats. For that question, the 23 October polls agree on the order: Yashar first, Likud second, Together third.

**A note on evidence gaps**

The simulation tried to collect first-person reactions from pollsters, journalists and 2022 Likud voters on how they read the outlier. The interview environment was unavailable, so this section relies only on the polls and media reports recorded in the simulation. Agents' own reasoning about the outlier is not captured here.

**What this means for the forecast**

The final poll picture shows a narrow Yashar edge that is consistent across six mainstream pollsters. Against it stand two pro-Likud outlets with a track record of overstating Likud, and a 2022 precedent of mainstream polls understating Likud by one to two seats. The gap is small enough that ordinary polling error, combined with Likud's more committed base and Yashar's voters drifting to Together, could reverse it. The mainstream polls were still unanimous at the blackout, which is why the simulation puts Likud's chance of winning the most seats at about 38%.

## How the Simulated Actors Behave: Framing Battles, Mobilization and Narrative Polarization

The simulated record does not include campaign speeches, rally transcripts or social-media posts by party operatives. An attempt to interview the simulated voters, pollsters and analysts directly failed because the simulation environment was no longer running. So this section reconstructs actor behavior from the traces the actors left in the record: how media outlets labeled each other's polls, which parties merged or withdrew, how firmly each voter group held its choice, and where voters moved. **No first-person campaign quotes are available.** The quotations below are the simulation's recorded facts, not agent statements.

**The framing battle runs through the polls themselves**

In this simulated campaign, the main contest over framing is about whose numbers count. The previous section set out the split between mainstream pollsters and the Channel 14/i24NEWS pair. What matters here is how the actors position themselves within that split. The simulated record links Channel 14's polling operation to the Netanyahu camp. It notes that "Jewish Insider reported on 22 October 2026 that Shlomo Filber runs Channel 14's polls" and that "Shlomo Filber was a former campaign aide to Benjamin Netanyahu."

The mainstream commentary in the simulation does two things at once. It labels the pro-Likud houses as outliers ("Jewish Insider called i24NEWS polls outliers"). It also keeps open the argument that the same houses were right last time: "On 22 October 2026, Jewish Insider noted that Direct Polls was regarded as the most accurate pollster of the previous election."

This leaves both camps with a usable story. Likud-aligned outlets can present a 26-to-30-seat Likud as the "real" picture that the 2022 track record supports. Mainstream outlets can present the six-poll Yashar consensus as settled and treat the outliers as house effects. Neither side in the simulation concedes the other's framing. That is why the gap between the two pictures persists into the blackout rather than narrowing.

**Opposition behavior: consolidation, but into two vessels rather than one**

The clearest strategic behavior in the simulation comes from the centre and centre-left. The record shows a series of consolidation moves over the year:

- "Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid merged Bennett 2026 and Yesh Atid into one list, Together (Beyachad), on 26 April 2026."
- "Chili Tropper merged his party into Yashar on 6 September 2026."
- "Benny Gantz pulled Blue and White out of the race on 19 October 2026 after polls kept showing the party below the 3.25% threshold."

The Gantz withdrawal, eight days before the vote, is the most consequential late move. The simulation links it directly to the final-week movement in the polls:

> "On 22 October 2026, JC linked a widening of the opposition lead to the dispersal of Benny Gantz's voters after Blue and White quit the race on 19 October 2026."

The consolidation stops at two lists, however, and the anti-Netanyahu vote stays divided between Eisenkot and Bennett. The simulation already shows movement between the two at the very end:

> "Some of Yashar's soft support could still drift to its own allies; Together gained two seats in the Maariv poll of 23 October 2026."

For the question of which list finishes first, this is the key structural feature. Opposition voters behave as one bloc against Netanyahu, but they are not lined up behind one list. Every seat that moves from Yashar to Together is neutral for the bloc arithmetic. It still narrows Yashar's margin over Likud.

**Mobilization asymmetry: a firm Likud base against a soft opposition**

The strongest behavioral signal in the record is how differently the three main lists' voters commit to their choice:

> "A Walla poll published on 20 October 2026 found that 68% of Likud voters were completely sure of their vote, compared with 40% of Yashar voters and 23% of Together voters."

The simulation therefore shows an electorate split in its intensity. Likud's supporters behave as a mobilized, settled base. Most of Yashar's voters, and more than three-quarters of Together's, are still persuadable a week out. This works in two directions. Soft support is more likely to switch lists at the last moment, as the Yashar-to-Together drift shows. It is also more likely not to turn out. Turnout among the soft voters is the main way the final mainstream polls could understate Likud's relative position. The scale is large: "The Central Elections Committee reported on 20 October 2026 that about 7.3 million Israelis are eligible to vote." Some voting is already under way: "Soldiers began early voting on 20 October 2026 at about 850 military polling stations."

**Polarization inside the right: Likud's base is firm but shrinking at the edges**

Likud's committed core sits alongside steady losses at both edges of its 2022 coalition of voters. Earlier in the year the simulation records leakage toward the centre:

> "A Channel 12 poll reported on 11 May 2026 found that 58% of 2022 Likud voters planned to vote Likud again."

> "A Channel 12 poll reported on 11 May 2026 found that 10% of 2022 Likud voters planned to vote Together."

> "A Channel 12 poll reported on 11 May 2026 found that 6% of 2022 Likud voters planned to vote Yashar."

The sharper late-campaign signal is a move to Likud's right:

> "Haaretz reported on 23 October 2026 that 56% of religious Zionists who voted Likud in 2022 did not plan to vote Likud this time and were leaning toward Itamar Ben-Gvir's or Bezalel Smotrich's parties."

This is narrative polarization showing up in voting behavior. Part of Likud's former electorate is radicalizing toward its coalition partners, and another part is moving to the centrist challengers. In bloc terms the rightward leakage costs Netanyahu nothing. In the largest-list contest that this forecast resolves on, it costs Likud seats directly. Likud's own campaign incentive, keeping right-wing voters on its own list, therefore conflicts with its partners' interest in growing.

**The leadership frame and the bloc stalemate**

At the level of leaders, the simulated electorate leans toward the challenger: "In Maariv's poll of 23 October 2026, Gadi Eisenkot led Benjamin Netanyahu 49% to 41% as preferred prime minister." Yet the bloc picture remains unresolved. "Three TV polls on 23 October 2026 projected 50 seats for Netanyahu's bloc," and neither bloc reached 61 seats in those polls. With no clear outcome on governing, the symbolic prize of finishing first becomes the main narrative trophy for both camps on election night. This raises the stakes of the framing battle over the exit polls. The record is explicit that "TV exit polls are released at 22:00 on the night of 27 October 2026 and are projections, not results."

**The threshold wildcard**

Small-party behavior adds noise that neither main camp controls. The simulation flags two lists near the line: "On 23 October 2026, Ofer Winter's party sat near the 3.25% electoral threshold," and so did "the Reservists," in each case "creating a risk that wasted votes could shift the larger lists by one or two seats." Gantz's exit has already shown how a threshold collapse redistributes votes. A similar fall below the line by either list could add seats to the larger lists by roughly the margin now separating Yashar and Likud.

**What the actor behavior implies for the forecast**

Taken together, the simulated actors produce pressures in both directions on Likud's chance of finishing first:

- **Toward Likud:** a far more committed base (68% certain against 40% for Yashar), soft opposition support that may switch or stay home, and continued leakage from Yashar to Together within the opposition.
- **Against Likud:** consolidation of the centrist vote after Gantz's exit, a lead for Eisenkot as preferred prime minister, and heavy defection of religious-Zionist 2022 Likud voters to Ben-Gvir and Smotrich.

None of these behaviors overturns the mainstream ordering on its own. Several of them, though, especially turnout differences and the drift between the two opposition lists, work through exactly the one-to-three-seat margins seen in the final polls. That is why the simulation treats Likud's bid for the largest list as a live minority outcome rather than a remote one.

## Key Swing Mechanisms That Could Flip the Outcome

The previous sections set out the final poll picture and how the campaign's actors behaved. This section asks what would have to happen between the blackout and the official count for Likud to overtake Yashar. In the final mainstream polls the gap is small: across the four polls published on 23 October, Yashar averages about 22 seats and Likud just under 20. The simulated record identifies several mechanisms that could close or widen that gap. Some favour Likud and some work against it. The attempt to interview simulated agents failed in an earlier section because the environment was no longer running, so this section relies on the recorded facts rather than first-person testimony.

**Mechanism 1: A repeat of the 2022 mainstream undercount of Likud**

The most direct route to a Likud plurality is a systematic polling miss in Likud's favour. The simulation records one precedent:

> "Mainstream pollsters underestimated Likud in the 2022 election: their final pre-election polls from 26–28 October 2022 gave Likud 30 or 31 seats, while Likud won 32 seats on 1 November 2022."

A one- to two-seat undercount is close to the size of Likud's deficit in this simulation, which the record states plainly:

> "In the three final TV polls of 23 October 2026, Likud trailed Yashar by 2 to 3 seats."

A 2022-sized error would therefore close most or all of the gap on its own, though probably not enough to win clearly. Moving from second to first by error alone needs an undercount of Likud combined with an overcount of Yashar, or a miss larger than the 2022 one. The simulation does not say whether the 2022 error came from a lasting methodological bias or from conditions specific to that election. This is the main source of uncertainty in the forecast.

**Mechanism 2: Differential turnout driven by vote certainty**

The previous section described the gap in how firmly each list's voters hold their choice. As a swing mechanism, it matters because it determines who actually votes:

> "A Walla poll published on 20 October 2026 found that 68% of Likud voters were completely sure of their vote, compared with 40% of Yashar voters and 23% of Together voters."

Pollsters' seat projections assume that stated preferences become ballots. When 60% of Yashar's projected voters are not fully committed, part of its 22-seat projection is soft support that could stay home, switch, or split. Likud's projection rests on a much firmer base. If uncommitted voters turn out less than committed ones, Likud's seat share should rise relative to its polls even if no one changes sides. Of the routes to YES in the record, this one most clearly depends on what happens on election day.

**Mechanism 3: Drift from Yashar to Together within the opposition**

The third mechanism needs no voter to cross from one bloc to the other. Yashar's lead over Likud can shrink if its soft support moves to its own ally:

> "Some of Yashar's soft support could still drift to its own allies; Together gained two seats in the Maariv poll of 23 October 2026."

Only 23% of Together's voters are fully committed, so movement between the two opposition lists can run either way. If Together's late gain continued, it would lower Yashar's ceiling without raising Likud's total. If Together's own uncommitted voters returned to Yashar in the final days to concentrate the anti-Netanyahu vote, Yashar's lead would widen. The simulation does not show which way this sorting ends. It does show that Yashar's first place depends on the opposition vote staying divided in roughly its current proportions.

**Mechanism 4: Small lists falling below the threshold**

The simulation names two parties at risk at the 3.25% threshold, both marked as possible swing factors:

> "On 23 October 2026, the Reservists sat near the 3.25% electoral threshold, creating a risk that wasted votes could shift the larger lists by one or two seats."

> "On 23 October 2026, Ofer Winter's party sat near the 3.25% electoral threshold, creating a risk that wasted votes could shift the larger lists by one or two seats."

One or two seats is the same size as the Yashar–Likud gap, so this mechanism alone could decide the outcome. The record does not say which bloc each of these parties draws from, or which major list would gain if either one passed or failed. The direction of the effect therefore cannot be determined from the simulation. The scale is clear, and it is large enough to change first place. The same logic was already visible a week earlier, when Benny Gantz withdrew Blue and White rather than risk wasted votes, and JC "linked a widening of the opposition lead to the dispersal of Benny Gantz's voters."

**Mechanism 5: Losses on Likud's right flank, a counter-mechanism**

Not every swing mechanism helps Likud. The simulation shows notable leakage to parties further right:

> "Haaretz reported on 23 October 2026 that 56% of religious Zionists who voted Likud in 2022 did not plan to vote Likud this time and were leaning toward Itamar Ben-Gvir's or Bezalel Smotrich's parties."

This works against Likud's chance of winning the most seats even though it does not hurt the right-wing bloc. Votes that go to Ben-Gvir or Smotrich count toward Netanyahu's bloc but not toward the Likud list's own total, and only that total decides the question. The earlier defection data point the same way:

> "A Channel 12 poll reported on 11 May 2026 found that 58% of 2022 Likud voters planned to vote Likud again."

The same Channel 12 poll found that 10% of 2022 Likud voters planned to vote Together and 6% Yashar. A late "vote Likud to finish first" appeal could bring some right-flank voters back. The simulation does not record such a campaign, so this possible recovery remains speculative.

**Mechanism 6: Momentum, which points both ways**

The trajectory of the race gives mixed signals. Likud had recovered over the autumn:

> "According to the JC average of 22 October 2026, Likud had recovered from about 17 seats in September 2026 to about 20 seats."

Earlier in October, mainstream outlets had shown the two lists level:

> "Zman Yisrael reported Likud and Yashar tied at 21 seats in a poll conducted on 30 September–1 October 2026."

> "Channel 13 reported Likud and Yashar tied at 20 seats on 7 October 2026."

By the final week, however, Channel 13 had moved from a tie to Yashar +3. The Gantz withdrawal appears to have helped Yashar more than Likud's slow recovery helped Likud. Whether the post-Gantz consolidation was still continuing when the blackout began, or had already peaked, cannot be observed. The polls stop, but the votes do not.

**Mechanism 7: Close-finish rules, late-counted ballots and the tie-break**

If the result is very close, procedure becomes decisive. A seat tie between Likud and Yashar goes to the list with more valid votes. A one-seat result could also change after election night, because some ballots are counted late:

> "Soldiers began early voting on 20 October 2026 at about 850 military polling stations."

> "Diplomatic staff abroad voted on 15–16 October 2026, and those ballots are counted after the regular count."

> "Official results must be published within eight days after election day, which means by 4 November 2026."

Election-night exit polls do not count toward the resolution. In a finish within one seat, the order on the night after the regular count could differ from the certified result. The simulation does not say which list the late-counted ballots favour. It does show a window between 27 October and 4 November in which a narrow first place could still change.

**How the mechanisms combine**

| Mechanism | Likely direction for Likud's chance of finishing first | Rough size in the record |
|---|---|---|
| Repeat of the 2022 mainstream undercount | Helps Likud | 1–2 seats |
| Vote-certainty and turnout gap | Helps Likud | Not quantified; 68% vs 40% certainty |
| Yashar → Together drift | Helps Likud (reduces the leader's total) | Together +2 in Maariv |
| Threshold failures (Reservists, Winter) | Unclear | 1–2 seats |
| Religious-Zionist leakage to Ben-Gvir and Smotrich | Hurts Likud | 56% of that group defecting |
| Post-Gantz consolidation | Hurts Likud | Credited with widening the lead |
| Late-counted ballots and tie-break | Unclear; matters only in a ≤1-seat finish | — |

Three mechanisms lean toward Likud: the polling-error precedent, the firmer Likud base, and drift within the opposition. Each is roughly the size of the gap. None is clearly large enough to produce a Likud lead by itself. On the other side, two documented forces keep pushing Likud's total down: right-flank leakage and the post-Gantz consolidation behind Yashar. Likud's realistic path to first place therefore needs at least two of the favourable mechanisms to operate together, for example a 2022-style undercount plus weaker-than-expected turnout among Yashar's uncommitted voters. That kind of combination is plausible but not the most likely outcome, which fits a probability below even but well above remote, in the high-30s range that the simulation as a whole produces.

## Probability Assessment and Forward-Looking Risks

The previous sections set out the final poll picture, how the campaign actors behaved, and the mechanisms that could move seats. This section turns that evidence into one number and flags the risks still open between the blackout and the certified result. A note on sources: the simulation environment could not be interviewed (see the earlier section), so this assessment rests on the recorded facts. The probability decomposition below is our own analytic synthesis of those facts. It is not a figure that appears in the simulation.

**Bottom line: about 38% that Likud wins the most seats**

The simulated future gives Likud a real but minority chance of finishing first. The core reasoning has three steps:

1. **The starting point is a modest Likud deficit.** Every mainstream poll in the final week has Likud second. The last-day TV polls are summarized as:

   > "In the three final TV polls of 23 October 2026, Likud trailed Yashar by 2 to 3 seats."

   The average deficit across the final mainstream polls is roughly two seats.

2. **That deficit is within the stated error bands.** The one poll that published a margin of error makes the point:

   > "The Maariv poll of 23 October 2026 stated a maximum sampling error of 3%."

   At the 19–23 seat level both lists sit at, a 3% error on each list's share corresponds to several seats. A two-seat gap between two lists is not a statistically secure lead.

3. **Known biases point in both directions.** The 2022 precedent (mainstream polls one to two seats low on Likud) and the turnout-certainty gap both favour Likud, as discussed in the previous section. Two other late signals in the simulation work against Likud. Both are new to this report.

**Signals that cap Likud's upside**

The first is a defection inside Likud's own 2022 coalition of voters, at the very end of the campaign:

> "Haaretz reported on 23 October 2026 that 56% of religious Zionists who voted Likud in 2022 did not plan to vote Likud this time and were leaning toward Itamar Ben-Gvir's or Bezalel Smotrich's parties."

This matters for the specific question asked here. Votes that move from Likud to Ben-Gvir or Smotrich stay inside Netanyahu's bloc, so they leave the bloc arithmetic unchanged. They still lower Likud's own seat count, and only that count resolves this question. Netanyahu's camp can therefore stay competitive as a bloc while losing ground in the race to be the single largest list. The defection also weakens part of the "firm base" argument. The 68% vote-certainty figure measures the voters Likud still holds. It says nothing about the voters it has already lost to its allies.

The second signal is the leadership contest, where the order matches the party polls:

> "In Maariv's poll of 23 October 2026, Gadi Eisenkot led Benjamin Netanyahu 49% to 41% as preferred prime minister."

An eight-point gap at the top of the ticket suggests Yashar's first place rests on more than a fragile list preference. Eisenkot himself is personally competitive.

**Signals that keep Likud in contention**

Against this, the trend over time shows a closer race than the final snapshot alone suggests. The simulation records a clear Likud recovery from its low point:

> "According to the JC average of 22 October 2026, Likud had recovered from about 17 seats in September 2026 to about 20 seats."

In early October, before Yashar's late widening, mainstream pollsters showed the race level:

> "Zman Yisrael reported Likud and Yashar tied at 21 seats in a poll conducted on 30 September–1 October 2026."

> "Channel 13 reported Likud and Yashar tied at 20 seats on 7 October 2026."

The race was therefore effectively tied about three weeks before the vote. The late Yashar edge is linked in the record to one event, the dispersal of Blue and White voters after Gantz withdrew on 19 October. Gains that come from a single late shock can be less durable than gains built up gradually. If part of that dispersed vote fails to turn out, or splits differently than pollsters assumed, the race could return to its early-October tie.

**How the number is built**

Taken together, the evidence gives the following assessment:

- **Mainstream consensus taken at face value** (Yashar +2): Likud's chance of finishing first is low, perhaps one in four, given normal sampling noise around a two-seat gap.
- **Adjustment for a repeat of the 2022 undercount and the turnout-certainty gap:** this pushes the estimate up substantially, toward even odds in the scenarios where both effects hold.
- **Adjustment for the religious Zionist defection, Eisenkot's leadership edge, and the uniformity of the mainstream polls** (25 Yashar leads among the 41 late polls, and every Likud lead coming from Channel 14 or i24NEWS): this pulls the estimate back down.
- **Weight given to the Direct Polls/Channel 14 picture:** small. Their 2022 record shows Likud-favourable overshoot as well as accuracy, and the Channel 14 operation is run by a former Netanyahu aide.

The net result is **roughly 38%**. Likud is the clear second favourite, well short of a coin flip, and a Likud plurality would be a moderate surprise rather than a shock.

**Forward-looking risks between the blackout and the official result**

Several procedural and timing factors in the simulation could affect how the question resolves, apart from the vote itself:

- **Exit polls are not the result.** The record is explicit:

  > "TV exit polls are released at 22:00 on the night of 27 October 2026 and are projections, not results."

  Exit polls at 22:00 on election night that show Likud first or second should not be treated as resolution. In a race separated by one or two seats, the exit-poll order could differ from the final count.

- **Late-counted ballots could reorder a tight finish.** Not every vote enters the main count:

  > "Diplomatic staff abroad voted on 15–16 October 2026, and those ballots are counted after the regular count."

  The record also notes that "Soldiers began early voting on 20 October 2026 at about 850 military polling stations." Where these ballots enter the count is not fully specified in the simulation. Any ballots counted after the regular count can move a seat at the margin, so an election-night "first place" decided by one seat stays provisional until the count is finished.

- **The certification timeline is fixed.** The resolution window is generous relative to the legal timetable:

  > "Article 11 of Basic Law: The Knesset requires official results to be published within eight days of election day, by 4 November 2026."

  This makes the 30 June 2027 cutoff very unlikely to bind. The risk of a "NO by default" because results are delayed is minimal.

- **A tie on seats is a real possibility.** Given the size of the gap and the two tied polls among the 41 late polls, a seat tie between Likud and Yashar cannot be ruled out. In that case the tie-breaker applies: "a tie in seats is broken by the number of valid votes for each list, and then by alphabetical order." Seat allocation by surplus agreements and rounding could leave the two lists level on seats while one leads on raw votes. Likud's more committed electorate might help it slightly in such a valid-vote tie-break, though the simulation offers no direct evidence on this.

- **The threshold gamble.** As noted earlier, the Reservists and Ofer Winter's party both sit near 3.25%. If either falls below the line, the redistribution "could shift the larger lists by one or two seats." That swing is the same size as the current gap, so this is one of the largest single sources of variance in the outcome.

**What would change the assessment**

Since no polls may be published before 22:00 on 27 October, the probability can only be updated from non-poll signals. Three would matter most. The first is turnout reports during the day that show heavy turnout in Likud-leaning areas relative to Yashar's softer, less committed base.
