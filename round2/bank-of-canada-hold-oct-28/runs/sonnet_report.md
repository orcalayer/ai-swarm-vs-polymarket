# Holding Steady at 2.25%: Simulated Forecast for the Bank of Canada's October 28, 2026 Rate Decision

> The simulation points to a very likely hold at 2.25% on October 28, 2026, with an estimated probability of about 92–94%, because forecasters broadly expect no move, the Bank has held for seven straight decisions, and the main remaining risk is a surprise data shock.

---

## Baseline State: A Seven-Meeting Hold Entering the Decision

The simulated world enters October 28, 2026 with a policy rate that has not moved in a year, an inflation backdrop pushed up by energy prices, and a labour market that is weakening. The baseline looks like a hold, but it is a hold under cross-pressure.

**The rate path behind the streak**

The simulation records the Bank of Canada's recent history as a full easing cycle that ended in a long pause:

> "The Bank of Canada last raised its overnight-rate target on 12 July 2023, to 5.00%, and held it through 10 April 2024; it then cut the rate at seven consecutive decisions from 5 June 2024 through 12 March 2025, reaching 2.75%, held in April, June, and July 2025, and cut by 25 basis points on 17 September 2025 to 2.50% and on 29 October 2025 to 2.25%."

Since that last cut, the rate has not moved:

> "The Bank of Canada kept its rate at 2.25% at seven consecutive decisions after the rate reached 2.25% on 29 October 2025; the latest of those decisions was on 2 September 2026."

At that September 2 decision the Bank held, but its language leaned hawkish. It "said on 2 September 2026 that the upside risks to inflation had increased" and that "it was prepared to adjust monetary policy as needed." The deliberations summary, released on September 16, added that "energy spillovers could require a monetary policy response." The same deliberations also recorded that the Governing Council "saw little evidence that gasoline costs were passing through to other prices."

**The decision setup**

The event is scheduled for 9:45 a.m. ET on Wednesday, October 28, and will be released with a new Monetary Policy Report. A press conference with Governor Tiff Macklem and Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers is set for about 10:30 a.m. The next scheduled decision is December 9, 2026. The last major data releases before the decision are the September jobs survey (October 9) and September CPI (October 19). The next CPI is not due until November 16, after the decision.

**An inflation picture with two faces**

Headline inflation is elevated, but the Bank's preferred core gauges are below target:

> "The CPI report of 19 October 2026 showed CPI-trim at 1.8%, CPI-median at 1.9%, CPI excluding gasoline at 2.2%, and headline CPI at 3.1%, below the 3.2% consensus."

Gasoline is the driver. Statistics Canada reported that gasoline prices were up 27.8% year over year. The energy backdrop is also still tight: "Reuters reported oil at US$117 on 23 October 2026," and the simulation records that "the Iran war disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz."

Macklem's pre-decision remarks point in the same direction:

> "On 17 October 2026, Tiff Macklem said the Bank of Canada expected to raise its inflation forecast because oil prices were far above the Bank of Canada's July assumption."

He also said that "if oil prices stayed around US$100, inflation was expected to gradually rise from 3 per cent," and that "the risk of inflation broadening had increased." Bank surveys released on October 19 reinforce the concern: "about half of firms planned to pass on cost increases, while nearly a quarter of firms could not say when inflation would return to 2%," and "consumers' long-term inflation expectations" were rising.

**A weakening real economy**

The labour market data cut the other way:

> "The survey of 9 October 2026 reported that employment fell by 42,000 in August and 68,000 in September, while unemployment rose to 6.5%."

Participation fell to 64.8%, and "average hourly wages were up 2.3% on the year." Growth is mixed. Second-quarter GDP grew at a 3.3% annualized pace, but monthly GDP was flat in July, with a flash estimate of 0.2% growth in August. Reuters-polled economists estimated "Canadian GDP growth of about 1.9% in the third quarter of 2026 and 1.3% in the fourth quarter."

Trade adds a further drag. After talks broke down on August 21, "the United States Government imposed new 50% tariffs affecting about US$20 billion of Canadian goods." On September 21, Macklem said that "if new US tariffs stay in place, fourth-quarter growth could be roughly halved to below 1%." The simulation also records that the Bank "said it did not want to be too slow to respond."

**External rate context**

Other central banks are tightening. The simulation records that "the US Federal Reserve lifted its range to 3.75%-4.00% on 16 September 2026." The ECB raised rates in June and again in September. The Canadian dollar firmed slightly, to 1.3993 per US dollar on October 23 from 1.4097 on October 19. No simulated source suggests a currency shock large enough to force the Bank's hand.

**How forecasters read the baseline**

The simulated economists lean toward a hold on October 28 and treat any hike as a later risk.

> "On 19 October 2026, RBC expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move."

Sherry Cooper and Peter Shannon likewise expected no move on the same date. Standard Chartered (October 20), Continuum Economics (October 21) and RBC again (October 22) all expected a hold. After the CPI release, TD's Leslie Preston said "the data supports the view that the Bank of Canada can remain on hold in 2026." BMO's Douglas Porter said the CPI report "did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes." CIBC's Andrew Grantham is the most hawkish voice on record, but even he only said policymakers "had time before raising rates, although a first hike could come before January 2027, when Andrew Grantham had forecast it would occur."

The simulation also records that a rate cut "looked remote because no forecast reviewed from 9–23 October 2026 called for one," even though job losses, tariffs and sub-2% core inflation would be arguments for one.

Some of the polling data is hard to read. The Reuters poll of October 21–23 is recorded as showing that "15 of 34 economists did not forecast a Bank of Canada rate hike," but other records tie similar poll numbers to the ECB. Derek Holt's "not a slam-dunk" hike comment and Desjardins' switch to a hike call are also recorded as ECB calls. I therefore treat these items as weak evidence on the Bank of Canada. They do not show a Canadian hike expected on October 28, and the horizon of any hike forecast is not specified.

**Limits of this baseline**

An attempt to interview simulated agents, including Macklem, Preston, Holt, Porter and the Reuters panel, returned no responses because the simulation environment was not running. The baseline above therefore rests on recorded facts and statements, not on fresh first-person testimony. In the record, the pieces that matter most for the October 28 hold are the unanimous no-move calls from the named forecasters, the absence of any cut forecast, and the core inflation readings below 2%. The two main risks to the hold are the hawkish tone from Macklem and the Bank's September language, and the possibility of a surprise in the Monetary Policy Report.

## Agent Reactions: Consensus Expectation of No Change

Agent behaviour in the simulation points to a near-unanimous expectation of a hold on October 28. The only disagreement concerns how soon a hike might come, not whether one comes at this meeting. Two evidence gaps limit how far this can be pushed: the live interviews failed, and the simulation holds no market-pricing data.

**Evidence gaps**

The interview run could not reach any simulated agents. The tool returned: "Interview failed: the simulation environment is not running or has been closed." The agent views below therefore come from recorded forecasts and statements, not first-person interview answers. The simulation also holds no figures for overnight index swaps, rate futures or bond-implied odds. The consensus is drawn from named forecasters, not from a market-implied probability.

**Bank economists and analysts: a hold, repeatedly stated**

Forecasters across banks, consultancies and independents recorded the same call in the days before the decision. RBC stated it on two dates:

> "On 19 October 2026, RBC expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move."

> "RBC still expected an interest rate hold on 22 October 2026."

The word "still" in the second entry shows RBC's call surviving the CPI release and the following week unchanged. Other forecasters landed in the same place:

- Standard Chartered: "expected an interest rate hold on 20 October 2026."
- Continuum Economics: "expected an interest rate hold on 21 October 2026."
- Peter Shannon of KPMG: "expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move" on 19 October.
- Sherry Cooper of Dominion Lending: "expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move" on 19 October.

**The September CPI as a reinforcing signal**

The 19 October CPI release reinforced the hold view. Headline inflation came in at 3.1%, below the 3.2% consensus, and the core gauges eased. TD's Leslie Preston read the data as supporting a long pause:

> "After the 19 October 2026 CPI release, TD's Leslie Preston said the data supports the view that the Bank of Canada can remain on hold in 2026."

BMO's Douglas Porter went further, saying the report weakened the case for tightening:

> "On 19 October 2026, Douglas Porter said the CPI report did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes."

**The hawkish minority: a later hike, not an October one**

CIBC's Andrew Grantham was the most hawkish named voice on the Bank of Canada. Even he pushed back against an immediate move:

> "On 19 October 2026, CIBC's Andrew Grantham said Bank of Canada policymakers had time before raising rates, although a first hike could come before January 2027, when Andrew Grantham had forecast it would occur."

His own forecast puts the first hike in January 2027, after the October 28 and December 9 decisions. He allows that it could come sooner, but he says policymakers have time. That is the clearest recorded sign that the hawkish side of the debate is not aiming at October 28.

**The cut side is thin**

The simulation also records the opposite tail as remote:

> "User said a rate cut looked remote because no forecast reviewed from 9–23 October 2026 called for one, although job losses, tariffs, and sub-2% core inflation would be arguments for a cut."

So the weak labour market, the tariff drag and core measures below 2% are acknowledged as arguments for easing. Even so, none of the forecasts reviewed over those two weeks called for a cut.

**Two readings that need caution**

- **The Reuters "15 of 34" figure.** The simulation records that "the Reuters poll conducted from 21 to 23 October 2026 found that 15 of 34 economists did not forecast an interest rate hike," and a separate entry repeats it as "15 did not forecast a Bank of Canada rate hike." The same poll is also recorded as showing "19 of 34 economists expected the ECB to raise rates by 25 basis points to 2.50%." The two numbers are exact complements (19 + 15 = 34). The 15 therefore most likely belongs to the ECB question, and the Bank of Canada label looks like a mislabelled retrieval. This report does not treat it as evidence of a split on the Canadian decision. The named Canadian forecasters above show no such split.
- **Other hike calls in the record.** BMO, Desjardins and Scotiabank's Derek Holt appear in the simulation with hike calls dated October 19. The recorded text ties them to the ECB, for example "Derek Holt said an ECB hike was not a slam-dunk call." They are not Bank of Canada forecasts.

**The Bank's own communication**

The Bank's most recent signals are consistent with a hold, though they lean hawkish. On October 17, Macklem said the Bank expected to raise its inflation forecast because oil was far above its July assumption. He added that "the risk of inflation broadening had increased." Raising a forecast and warning about risks is different from signalling a move at this meeting, and no forecaster in the record read it as one. The Governing Council had also "saw little evidence that gasoline costs were passing through to other prices" at its September 2 deliberations.

**What the reactions amount to**

Every named Bank of Canada forecast in the simulation expects no change on October 28: RBC, Standard Chartered, Continuum Economics, KPMG's Shannon and Dominion Lending's Cooper. TD's Preston extends that to the rest of 2026. The two voices who lean toward higher rates, Grantham and the unnamed hawkish minority, place any hike after this meeting. The residual risk is that the new Monetary Policy Report and the Bank's inflation language prove sharper than forecasters expect, since oil is still at US$117 and firms and consumers are signalling rising inflation expectations. Nothing in the recorded agent behaviour shows anyone forecasting that.

## Risk Scenarios: What Could Break the Hold

The simulation contains no agent, forecaster or data series that predicts a move on October 28. The risks below are therefore tail scenarios built from pressures the simulation records. The simulation assigns no probabilities to them. Two limits on the evidence apply. The live interviews failed earlier, and no market-pricing data exists. The scenarios rest on recorded statements and data, not on first-person agent testimony.

**Scenario 1: a hawkish surprise (the more plausible break)**

The Bank's own recent language gives a hike the most support. After the September 2 hold, the Bank stated:

> "The Bank said on 2 September 2026 that it was prepared to adjust monetary policy as needed."

The deliberations summary went further, and the Bank added later that it wanted to avoid being late:

> "The Bank's deliberations on 2 September 2026 said energy spillovers could require a monetary policy response."

> "On 21 September 2026, the Bank said it did not want to be too slow to respond."

The same deliberations were also cautious about pass-through. The Governing Council "saw little evidence that gasoline costs were passing through to other prices" and "judged that the economy was still in excess supply." A hike would require the Bank to reverse that judgement quickly.

The simulation records several pressure points that could push it that way:

- **Oil far above the Bank's assumption.** Macklem said on October 17 that the Bank "expected to raise its inflation forecast because oil prices were far above the Bank of Canada's July assumption." The simulation records that The Hub reported the July outlook assumed about US$75 oil, and Reuters recorded oil at US$117 on October 23.
- **Broadening risk.** Macklem said "the risk of inflation broadening had increased." Bank surveys released October 19 showed "about half of firms planned to pass on cost increases" and "consumers' long-term inflation expectations rising."
- **Foreign central banks tightening.** The simulation records that "the Fed hiked rates on 16 September 2026," lifting its range to 3.75%–4.00%. It also records two ECB hikes in 2026, effective June 17 and September 16.

Several recorded facts weaken this scenario:

- The core gauges eased in the last CPI. CPI-trim fell to 1.8% and CPI-median to 1.9%.
- Headline CPI came in below consensus.
- The Canadian dollar firmed, from 1.4097 per US dollar on October 19 to 1.3993 on October 23. The simulation does not say how that move bears on the Bank's decision.
- The most hawkish named Canadian forecaster, CIBC's Andrew Grantham, still has time on his side:

> "On 19 October 2026, CIBC's Andrew Grantham said Bank of Canada policymakers had time before raising rates, although a first hike could come before January 2027, when Andrew Grantham had forecast it would occur."

BMO's Douglas Porter reached a similar verdict on the data:

> "On 19 October 2026, Douglas Porter said the CPI report did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes."

**A caution on the "hike calls"**

The simulation records that "BMO switched to hike calls on 19 October 2026" and that Desjardins "switched to an ECB rate-hike call" the same day. One BMO entry does not name a central bank. Other entries explicitly tie the BMO and Desjardins switches to the ECB. The simulation also records Scotiabank's Derek Holt saying "an ECB hike was not a slam-dunk call but that there was probably enough supporting evidence for a hike." The evidence does not support reading these as Bank of Canada hike calls for October 28. They show a global tightening backdrop, not a domestic forecaster defection.

**Scenario 2: a dovish surprise (a thinner but real tail)**

The case for a cut rests on the real economy. The labour data is the clearest recorded weakness:

> "The survey of 9 October 2026 reported that employment fell by 42,000 in August and 68,000 in September, while unemployment rose to 6.5%."

Wages grew only 2.3% and participation fell to 64.8%. The trade shock adds to it. After talks broke down on August 21, the US imposed new 50% tariffs affecting about US$20 billion of Canadian goods. Macklem has already framed the growth risk:

> "On 21 September 2026, Governor Tiff Macklem said that if new US tariffs stay in place, fourth-quarter growth could be roughly halved to below 1%."

Monthly GDP was flat in July. Reuters-polled economists see third-quarter growth near 1.9% and fourth-quarter growth near 1.3%.

The simulation also records that these arguments have not changed any forecast:

> "User said a rate cut looked remote because no forecast reviewed from 9–23 October 2026 called for one, although job losses, tariffs, and sub-2% core inflation would be arguments for a cut."

Oil near US$117 makes a cut hard to justify while the Bank is warning about inflation spillovers. A cut would also contradict its own recent hawkish language.

**Scenario 3: a shock arriving after October 24**

The data calendar leaves little room for new information. The September jobs survey and September CPI were "among the last major data before the decision," and the next CPI is not due until November 16. A break would therefore have to come from an unscheduled event.

- **An oil or geopolitical move.** The simulation links the Iran war to disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and records oil that peaked near US$120 in April. A sharp change in either direction before the decision would change the Monetary Policy Report's forecast.
- **A tariff escalation or reversal.** A new US action, or a restart of the talks that collapsed on August 21, would alter the growth outlook the Bank must weigh.
- **The Monetary Policy Report itself.** The report will be released with the decision, and the simulation records that Macklem expects to raise the inflation forecast. A higher forecast with an unchanged rate would be consistent with a hold. It would only imply a move if the Bank decided its forecasts required action now.

**Procedural paths that do not break the hold**

The question's rules shield the "YES" outcome from several disruptions:

- A postponed announcement counts if it comes before the December 9 decision.
- A cancelled decision, or no decision before December 9, resolves as no change.
- Any emergency move outside the October 28 announcement is ignored.
- A move of any size counts as a change, since sub-25-basis-point moves are rounded to 25.

The result is asymmetric. Only an actual hike or cut at the October 28 announcement resolves the question as NO.

**Net read of the risk map**

The tail risks point in opposite directions. The hawkish tail rests on the Bank's warning language and on oil prices far above its July assumption. The dovish tail rests on job losses and tariffs, which the Bank has so far treated as a reason for patience. Both tails sit against the consensus recorded in the previous section, in which every named forecaster expected a hold and the most hawkish pushed a hike to January 2027 or later. The simulation records no forecast, official statement or data release that points to a move on October 28. It supports describing the hold as likely, with a surprise requiring a fresh shock, but it does not quantify that shock.

## Final Probability Assessment

The simulation puts the probability of "no change" at the October 28, 2026 announcement at **about 93%**, inside the 92–94% range in the report summary. The call rests on recorded forecasts, recorded data and the Bank's own communications. It does not rest on market pricing or on first-person agent interviews, because the simulation holds neither (see the evidence limits below).

**What the evidence says**

Three lines of evidence point to a hold. None of them points to a move.

1. **Named forecasters agree.** Every Bank of Canada call recorded in the simulation is a hold. RBC, Standard Chartered, Continuum Economics, KPMG's Peter Shannon and Dominion Lending's Sherry Cooper all expected no change. TD and BMO read the September CPI as lending no support to tightening:

> "After the 19 October 2026 CPI release, TD's Leslie Preston said the data supports the view that the Bank of Canada can remain on hold in 2026."

> "On 19 October 2026, Douglas Porter said the CPI report did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes."

2. **The hawkish voice is not aiming at October.** The most hawkish named forecaster, CIBC's Andrew Grantham, dates his first hike to January 2027. He says policymakers have time:

> "On 19 October 2026, CIBC's Andrew Grantham said Bank of Canada policymakers had time before raising rates, although a first hike could come before January 2027, when Andrew Grantham had forecast it would occur."

3. **The data does not force a move either way.** Headline CPI of 3.1% is high, but it came in below the 3.2% consensus. The Bank's preferred core gauges are below target: CPI-trim at 1.8% and CPI-median at 1.9%. Employment fell by 68,000 in September, and unemployment rose to 6.5%. These readings make a hike hard to justify now. They also stop short of the conditions that would justify an immediate cut, and the simulation records that no forecast reviewed from October 9 to 23 called for one.

**Why not higher than 93%**

The 7% residual reflects a credible but thin hawkish tail. The Bank has spent weeks signalling vigilance. It said on September 2 that it was prepared to adjust policy as needed, and the later record shows it did not want to be too slow to respond. Macklem said on October 17 that the Bank "expected to raise its inflation forecast because oil prices were far above the Bank of Canada's July assumption," and that "the risk of inflation broadening had increased." Oil was at US$117 on October 23. The October 28 decision also arrives with a new Monetary Policy Report, which gives the Bank a natural moment to change course. Foreign central banks have already tightened: the simulation records a Fed hike on September 16 and two ECB hikes in 2026.

A cut carries less weight than a hike in this residual. The weak labour market and tariff drag are real, but they are not matched by any recorded cut forecast.

**How the resolution rules affect the number**

The rules add almost nothing to the NO side:

- Any move of any size, up or down, counts as NO.
- A postponed announcement counts if it is made before December 9.
- A cancelled announcement, or no decision before December 9, counts as YES.
- An emergency move outside the October 28 announcement is ignored.

A postponement, a cancellation or an emergency move is not mentioned as a live scenario anywhere in the simulation. The rules therefore leave the estimate essentially where the policy-rate evidence puts it.

**Evidence limits that argue against going to 97–99%**

- **The Reuters figure is ambiguous.** The simulation records that "of 34 economists surveyed, 15 did not forecast a Bank of Canada rate hike." The same poll shows that "19 of 34 economists expected the ECB to raise rates by 25 basis points to 2.50%," and 19 + 15 = 34. The 15 therefore most likely belongs to the ECB question. Read literally as a Bank of Canada number, it would mean 19 of 34 economists forecast a Canadian hike, which contradicts every named Canadian forecaster in the simulation. I give this figure no weight in either direction.
- **The BMO and Desjardins "hike calls" concern the ECB.** The simulation ties both switches to the ECB, so they are not Canadian defections.
- **There is no market-implied probability.** The simulation holds no overnight index swaps, rate futures or bond-implied odds, which would be the best check on a forecaster consensus.
- **No outcome is recorded.** A search for the October 28 result returned only scheduling facts and resolution rules, so the forecast cannot be checked against an actual decision.
- **The interviews failed.** The agent views come from recorded statements, not live answers.

**Bottom line**

The simulated world converges on a hold. The Bank has held for seven straight decisions. All named Canadian forecasters called for no change. Core inflation is below target and the labour market is deteriorating, while headline inflation is driven by gasoline and the hawkish risk is real but deferred. A surprise on October 28 would most likely be a hawkish one, produced by the oil shock and a more forceful Monetary Policy Report. Without any forecaster or data series pointing that way, the probability stays modest.

**Final estimate: 93% that the Bank of Canada leaves the target for the overnight rate unchanged at 2.25% on October 28, 2026.**
