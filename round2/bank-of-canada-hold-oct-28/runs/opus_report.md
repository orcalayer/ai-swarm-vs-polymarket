# Holding Pattern at 2.25%: Simulated Outlook for the Bank of Canada's October 28, 2026 Rate Decision

> The simulation points strongly toward the Bank of Canada leaving its overnight rate target unchanged at 2.25% on October 28, 2026, with an estimated probability of about 88–92%. Its eighth straight hold is backed by a near-unanimous expectation among economists and institutions in the days before the announcement.

---

## Core Prediction: An Eighth Consecutive Hold at 2.25%

The simulation projects that on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, the Bank of Canada leaves its target for the overnight rate at **exactly 2.25%**. That would be its eighth consecutive hold since the last cut on October 29, 2025. We put the probability of a "no change" outcome at **about 88–92%**. The remaining 8–12% is weighted mostly toward a surprise hike. A cut is very unlikely.

**The headline call: a hold that almost everyone saw coming**

In the days before the announcement, nearly every forecaster tracked in the simulation expected the Bank to stay put. The simulated record shows these calls building through the final week:

> "On 19 October 2026, RBC expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move."

> "On 19 October 2026, Sherry Cooper expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move."

> "On 19 October 2026, Peter Shannon expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move."

> "Standard Chartered expected an interest rate hold on 20 October 2026."

> "Continuum Economics expected an interest rate hold on 21 October 2026."

> "RBC still expected an interest rate hold on 22 October 2026."

The hold camp covers several kinds of forecaster:

- **Big-bank research:** RBC, TD and CIBC.
- **Mortgage-market voices:** Sherry Cooper of Dominion Lending.
- **Advisory firms:** Peter Shannon of KPMG.
- **International houses:** Standard Chartered and Continuum Economics.

RBC repeated its call on October 22, which suggests the consensus was not just a reaction to one data release. It held through the final pre-decision data.

**Why the data led forecasters to "wait"**

In the simulation, the decisive input was the September CPI report released on October 19. It was one of the last major data releases before the decision. Headline inflation looked uncomfortable, but it still came in below expectations, and every underlying measure cooled:

> "The CPI report of 19 October 2026 showed CPI-trim at 1.8%, CPI-median at 1.9%, CPI excluding gasoline at 2.2%, and headline CPI at 3.1%, below the 3.2% consensus."

Big-bank economists read this as a reason to delay any tightening:

> "After the 19 October 2026 CPI release, TD's Leslie Preston said the data supports the view that the Bank of Canada can remain on hold in 2026."

> "On 19 October 2026, Douglas Porter said the CPI report did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes."

> "On 19 October 2026, CIBC's Andrew Grantham said Bank of Canada policymakers had time before raising rates, although a first hike could come before January 2027, when Andrew Grantham had forecast it would occur."

Grantham's comment captures the mood behind the forecast. The question in the simulated world is **when** the Bank tightens, not **whether** it eases. Even so, the near-term answer is "not in October."

The labour market adds a second reason for patience:

> "The survey of 9 October 2026 reported that employment fell by 42,000 in August and 68,000 in September, while unemployment rose to 6.5%."

On top of that, wages grew only 2.3% year over year and participation fell to 64.8%. Raising rates into back-to-back monthly job losses would be very hard to justify, and that is the main force keeping the Bank on hold.

**Why the risk tilts toward a hike, not a cut**

The forecast is not risk-free. The simulation shows the Bank's own messaging turning more hawkish. Governor Tiff Macklem spoke at IMF meetings in Bangkok on October 17:

> "On 17 October 2026, Tiff Macklem said the Bank of Canada expected to raise its inflation forecast because oil prices were far above the Bank of Canada's July assumption."

> "On 17 October 2026, Tiff Macklem said the risk of inflation broadening had increased."

Several other signals point the same way:

- **The Bank's guidance:** its September 2 statement already said "the upside risks to inflation had increased" and that it "was prepared to adjust monetary policy as needed."
- **Its deliberations:** the record noted that "energy spillovers could require a monetary policy response."
- **The oil gap:** the July outlook reportedly assumed oil of about US$75. Reuters reported oil at US$117 on October 23.
- **Survey evidence:** surveys released on October 19 showed "about half of firms planned to pass on cost increases," and "consumers' long-term inflation expectations rising."

These are the ingredients of a hawkish surprise, and they explain most of the 8–12% that is not a hold. The new October Monetary Policy Report, with an inflation forecast the Governor has already said will rise, is the obvious place for such a turn.

A cut, by contrast, looks almost off the table. In the simulation, the person who set the question summarized it like this:

> "A rate cut looked remote because no forecast reviewed from 9–23 October 2026 called for one, although job losses, tariffs, and sub-2% core inflation would be arguments for a cut."

The arguments for a cut do exist. The US imposed new 50% tariffs on about US$20 billion of Canadian goods, and Macklem had warned that fourth-quarter growth could be roughly halved. But in the simulated world, the oil-driven inflation threat outweighs them.

**A caveat on the polling evidence**

Some poll evidence in the simulation needs careful handling. The record contains the line "Of 34 economists surveyed, 15 did not forecast a Bank of Canada rate hike." However, the same Reuters poll from October 21–23 is elsewhere described as "19 of 34 economists expected the ECB to raise rates by 25 basis points to 2.50%." The numbers match exactly: 19 + 15 = 34. So the "15 of 34" figure most likely comes from the European Central Bank poll and was wrongly attributed to the Bank of Canada.

The same applies to two other records:

- Derek Holt's remark that a hike was "not a slam-dunk call" explicitly refers to the ECB.
- The records that "BMO switched to hike calls" and "Desjardins switched to an ECB rate-hike call" on October 19 appear to belong to the same European context.

BMO's own Douglas Porter said the same day that Canadian CPI "did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes." For these reasons, our estimate does **not** read the poll as showing that 19 of 34 economists expected a Bank of Canada hike. We do treat this ambiguity as a source of uncertainty and as one reason the forecast stops short of 95%.

The simulation also lacks direct market pricing for the Bank of Canada, such as overnight index swap–implied probabilities. Our attempt to interview simulated agents (including the Governor, Scotiabank's Holt and CIBC's Grantham) failed because the simulation environment was offline. As a result, this forecast rests on the recorded public statements and data, not on first-person interviews.

**How the settlement rules support the estimate**

The resolution rules lift the "YES" probability slightly. If the announcement is cancelled, or no decision is made before December 9, the outcome counts as a hold, and emergency moves outside the October 28 announcement are ignored. The simulation shows the event proceeding as scheduled: the decision is at 9:45 a.m. ET, and Governor Macklem and Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers hold a press conference at about 10:30 a.m. ET. On the other side, the rule that any move rounds to at least 25 basis points means even a small "signalling" adjustment would count as NO. The Bank sets a single target rate, so there is no room for a partial outcome.

**Putting it together**

The simulated world on the eve of the decision has these features:

- Core inflation is below 2%, and headline CPI came in lower than expected.
- Employment fell by 110,000 jobs over two months.
- A Governor openly worries about oil-driven inflation spreading.
- Forecasters agree on a hold, with no recorded expectation of a cut.

On balance, the simulation projects that the Bank of Canada holds at 2.25% on October 28. The policy statement and Monetary Policy Report are likely to sound noticeably more hawkish, which sets up December 9 or early 2027 as the real debate over a hike. Our final estimate for the probability that the overnight rate target is unchanged is **88–92%**.

## Agent Consensus: Banks and Economists Converge on 'No Move'

The simulation shows the forecasting community agreeing on the October 28 decision but not on what follows. Every forecaster in the record expects the Bank to stay at 2.25% this month. Where they differ is on how long the hold lasts and how soon the first hike arrives. This chapter looks at how that agreement is built, where it breaks down, and what that means for our 88–92% hold probability.

**Nobody in the record forecasts a cut**

The most important finding for the forecast is something the record does not contain. No forecaster calls for a cut. The scenario's own assessment says so directly:

> "A rate cut looked remote because no forecast reviewed from 9–23 October 2026 called for one, although job losses, tariffs, and sub-2% core inflation would be arguments for a cut."

So the simulation does contain arguments for easing:

- two months of job losses
- the 50% US tariffs imposed after trade talks broke down on August 21
- CPI-trim and CPI-median both below 2%

Yet no agent in the simulated world turns those arguments into a forecast. That effectively closes off one of the two ways the outcome could resolve NO. All the remaining risk sits on the hike side.

**A coalition that holds together through the final data**

The previous chapter listed the individual hold calls. Two features of those calls matter more for judging how solid the consensus is.

**First, the calls come from different kinds of forecasters.** The hold camp includes:

- domestic bank research desks (RBC, TD, CIBC)
- a mortgage-market economist (Sherry Cooper of Dominion Lending)
- a Big Four advisory firm (Peter Shannon of KPMG)
- two international houses (Standard Chartered and Continuum Economics)

These groups work for different audiences and use different models, so their agreement is unlikely to come from a shared bias.

**Second, the calls cluster after the last major data releases.** The record notes that both the September jobs survey (October 9) and September CPI (October 19) were "among the last major data before the decision." Most hold calls are dated October 19–22. The forecasters therefore made their calls after seeing the final major data, not before. The next CPI release is not scheduled until after the decision:

> "The next CPI is scheduled for 16 November 2026, after the decision mentioned in the source text."

There is little scheduled news left that could change the consensus before 9:45 a.m. on October 28.

**The Reuters poll: a hold now, a hike debate later**

The most informative piece of new evidence is the Reuters poll conducted October 21–23:

> "The Reuters poll conducted from 21 to 23 October 2026 found that 15 of 34 economists did not forecast an interest rate hike."

On its face, this means a majority of 34 economists (19 of them) had a hike somewhere in their forecasts. That could look like it contradicts the near-unanimous hold expectation. It does not. The figure appears to describe hike expectations over the forecast horizon, not a call for a move on October 28. None of the named forecasters calls for an October hike. CIBC's Andrew Grantham's framing, quoted in the previous chapter, has the hike debate centred on around January 2027 or "before" that.

A caveat applies. The simulated record also links a "19 of 34" figure from the same poll window to the ECB rather than the Bank of Canada:

> "A Reuters poll conducted 21–23 October 2026 found that 19 of 34 economists expected the ECB to raise rates by 25 basis points to 2.50%, reversing the previous poll."

The matching numbers suggest that some poll results for different central banks got mixed together in the simulation's knowledge graph. We therefore treat the "15 of 34" figure as **directional evidence**. It shows that a significant share of economists expect Canadian tightening in the months ahead. It does not show a split over the October decision itself.

The same poll gives the growth backdrop forecasters are working with:

> "Economists polled by Reuters on 21–23 October 2026 estimated Canadian GDP growth of about 1.9% in the third quarter of 2026 and 1.3% in the fourth quarter."

A fourth-quarter slowdown to 1.3% is consistent with a Bank that waits for its new Monetary Policy Report projections before moving.

**Hawkish signals at the edges of the consensus**

The record includes a small group of more hawkish signals, all dated October 19, the day of the CPI release:

> "BMO switched to hike calls on 19 October 2026."

> "Desjardins switched to an ECB rate-hike call on 19 October 2026."

> "On 19 October 2026, Scotiabank economist Derek Holt said an ECB hike was not a slam-dunk call but that there was probably enough supporting evidence for a hike."

These facts come with the same ambiguity as the Reuters figure. The simulation labels them as ECB calls, but they come from Canadian institutions. The record also gives a parallel version for BMO and Holt without the ECB label:

> "BMO switched to an ECB rate-hike call on 19 October 2026."

> Holt's entity summary: "On 19 October 2026, he said a rate hike was not a 'slam dunk' but that there was probably enough supporting evidence for one."

The BMO case contains an internal contradiction. On the same day, BMO's Douglas Porter is recorded as saying:

> "the CPI report did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes."

The most coherent reading is this. Some Canadian desks (BMO, Desjardins, and possibly Scotiabank) moved to forecasting a hiking cycle on October 19. Their language still describes a case that is building, not an action they expect next week. Holt's "not a slam dunk" is the strongest hawkish statement anywhere in the record, and even it stops short of calling for an October move. These institutions do not break the hold consensus. They are the forecasters most likely to change their minds quickly if the October 28 Monetary Policy Report shows a sharply higher inflation path.

**How the consensus fits the forecast probability**

Taken together, the agent behaviour supports a probability in the high 80s to low 90s, not something closer to certainty:

1. **A cut is effectively ruled out.** No forecaster calls for one.
2. **An October hike is not anyone's base case.** The hawkish shifts on October 19 point to tightening later, with Grantham's January 2027 timeline as the reference point.
3. **The residual risk is real but small.** A large and possibly growing share of economists expect hikes eventually. Governor Macklem has signalled a higher inflation forecast. The October decision also comes with a full Monetary Policy Report, which historically is when the Bank is most willing to change course. These factors keep a meaningful tail risk of an early hike that the consensus has not priced in.

**Data limitations**

The planned interviews with simulated forecasters (Sherry Cooper, Derek Holt, Andrew Grantham, Leslie Preston, the Reuters panel and a forecasting analyst) did not take place because the simulation environment had shut down. This chapter therefore relies only on the recorded statements and events in the simulation's knowledge graph. It contains no first-person interview material. The sample of named forecasters is also small, and some poll results appear to be mixed up between the Bank of Canada and the ECB. The overall direction (unanimous on holding in October, divided on hikes afterwards) holds across every source. The exact size of the hawkish minority should be read with caution.

## Residual Risks: What Could Break the Hold

The earlier chapters show why a hold is the strong base case. This chapter asks the reverse question: what in the simulated world could still push the October 28 outcome to NO? The record points to one main hazard, an **energy-driven hawkish surprise**. It also points to a smaller **growth-collapse cut risk** and some **procedural edge cases**, and none of those edge cases is likely to change the result. We split the 8–12% non-hold probability roughly as follows:

- **About 7–10 points: a surprise hike.**
- **About 1–2 points: a surprise cut.**
- **Close to zero: procedural outcomes.**

A note on sources: we tried to interview simulation agents directly, including Governor Macklem, Derek Holt, Sherry Cooper, Andrew Grantham and the Reuters panel. The simulation environment was not running, so no interview transcripts exist. The analysis below relies only on the recorded facts in the simulated knowledge graph.

**Risk 1: Oil is well above what the Bank planned for**

The biggest danger to the hold is how far energy prices have moved from the Bank's own baseline. Macklem's October 17 remarks on revising the inflation forecast were covered earlier. What the record adds is how large that revision would be:

> "The Hub reported that the July 2026 outlook assumed an oil price of about US$75."

> "The Hub reported that Brent settled at US$113.17 on 22 October 2026."

> "Reuters reported oil at US$117 on 23 October 2026."

Oil at US$117 is more than 50% above the assumption behind the Bank's last full forecast. It is also close to the April peak:

> "The Bank of Canada wrote on 29 July 2026 that benchmark oil had peaked near US$120 a barrel in April 2026."

The record also links the energy shock to geopolitics, noting that "the Iran war disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz." Oil was still rising in the final trading days before the decision, from about US$113 Brent on October 22 to US$117 on October 23. That matters because Macklem's own conditional scenario assumed oil "stayed around US$100." By decision day, the actual price was well above even the Bank's updated reference point.

The pass-through to Canadian prices is already visible:

> "Statistics Canada reported on 19 October 2026 that gasoline prices were up 27.8% year over year in September 2026."

**Risk 2: The Bank's reason for patience may be eroding**

Before the September 2 hold, one belief underpinned the Bank's patience. The simulation records it as a fact that **has since expired**:

> "The Governing Council saw little evidence that gasoline costs were passing through to other prices during deliberations before the 2 September 2026 decision."

The same deliberations had set out what would trigger a response:

> "The Bank's deliberations on 2 September 2026 said energy spillovers could require a monetary policy response."

The survey evidence released on October 19 is the closest thing in the record to that trigger being hit. Core CPI measures were cooling, but expectations and pricing intentions were moving the wrong way:

> "Bank surveys released on 19 October 2026 showed about half of firms planned to pass on cost increases, while nearly a quarter of firms could not say when inflation would return to 2%."

> "Bank surveys released on 19 October 2026 showed consumers' long-term inflation expectations rising."

This creates the main tension behind the residual hike risk. The hard data (CPI-trim at 1.8% and CPI-median at 1.9%) say spillovers have not yet happened. The soft data on firms' pricing plans and consumers' long-term expectations say they may be starting. If the Governing Council decides the surveys matter more than the backward-looking core figures, it has already told markets it is "prepared to adjust monetary policy as needed." A pre-emptive hike would therefore not be out of character.

**Risk 3: A hawkish Monetary Policy Report on the same day**

The October decision comes with a new forecast, so new projections and the rate call arrive at the same moment:

> "The Bank of Canada will announce its October 2026 decision together with the October 2026 Monetary Policy Report."

A forecast round is where a central bank can most easily justify a change in direction. An upward inflation revision is already signalled, and a large one, built on oil near US$117 rather than US$75, could give the Governing Council internal cover to move. The press conference arrangements add some uncertainty about how the message will be delivered:

> "Governor Tiff Macklem will hold a press conference at about 10:30 a.m. ET on 28 October 2026."

> "Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers will hold a press conference at about 10:30 a.m. ET on 28 October 2026."

We read the more likely outcome as a **hawkish hold**: inflation forecasts raised and explicit tightening guidance, with the rate left at 2.25%. That outcome resolves YES. The residual risk is the smaller chance that the Bank skips the signalling step and moves immediately.

**Risk 4: Hawkish signals from abroad and from the currency**

Two weaker signals in the record lean the same way. First, the global environment is turning toward tightening. The ECB "raised its rates effective 17 June 2026" and "again effective 16 September 2026." In the days before the Canadian decision, forecasters were moving toward a further ECB hike:

> "Desjardins switched to an ECB rate-hike call on 19 October 2026."

> "On 19 October 2026, Scotiabank economist Derek Holt said an ECB hike was not a slam-dunk call but that there was probably enough supporting evidence for a hike."

Second, the Canadian dollar strengthened in the final week:

> "The Bank of Canada's daily rate was 1.3993 Canadian dollars per US dollar on 23 October 2026, compared with 1.4097 Canadian dollars per US dollar on 19 October 2026."

The record does not explain the currency move. A firmer loonie during an oil rally fits markets starting to price some Canadian tightening. It also slightly reduces imported inflation, which could make an October hike less urgent. We treat both signals as background colour, not as decisive.

**Risk 5: A growth collapse that forces a cut (very unlikely)**

The case for easing is real, and the record now adds details on growth beyond the labour data already discussed:

> "On 21 September 2026, Governor Tiff Macklem said that if new US tariffs stay in place, fourth-quarter growth could be roughly halved to below 1%."

> "Statistics Canada reported on 28 August 2026 that real Canadian GDP grew at a 3.3% annualized pace in the second quarter of 2026; by 29 September 2026, monthly GDP was flat in July and a flash estimate showed growth of 0.2% in August."

> "Economists polled by Reuters on 21–23 October 2026 estimated Canadian GDP growth of about 1.9% in the third quarter of 2026 and 1.3% in the fourth quarter."

The Bank's own September assessment, "the economy was still in excess supply," would technically support a cut. A cut would need the Governing Council to ignore its own warning about inflation upside, its signalled upward forecast revision, and oil at US$117, all at once. No agent in the simulation argues for that path. The only additional growth data that could have changed the picture also arrives too late:

> "The next jobs report is scheduled for 6 November 2026, after the decision mentioned in the source text."

We therefore keep the cut probability at about 1–2%, mainly as allowance for a sudden market or tariff shock in the final days.

**Risk 6: Procedural edge cases (close to zero)**

The resolution rules in the simulation largely neutralise timing risks:

> "User says a postponed 28 October 2026 announcement counts if it is made before the next scheduled decision on 9 December 2026."

> "User says an emergency move made outside the 28 October 2026 announcement does not count."

A cancellation resolves YES, and an emergency move outside the scheduled announcement is ignored. As a result, the only procedural route to NO is a postponed announcement that then delivers a change before December 9. A delay would most likely only happen during a crisis, and a crisis would in turn make a change more likely. Even so, there is no sign in the record of any postponement risk, so this route adds almost nothing to the overall probability.

**Bottom line for the forecast**

What could break the hold is not ambiguous in the simulated world. It is **a hike prompted by oil, triggered by the Bank concluding that energy costs have started to spread into expectations and pricing behaviour**. Each piece of that scenario appears in the record: oil far above the July assumption, firms planning to pass on costs, rising consumer inflation expectations, a deliberation that explicitly allowed for a policy response, and a new MPR released alongside the decision.

What keeps this a tail risk and not a coin flip is equally clear. Core inflation is below 2%, the economy shed jobs in both August and September, and every named forecaster expects the move to come later, not on October 28. The most likely way the hawkish pressure shows up is in the **language and forecasts** published at 9:45 a.m. ET, not in the rate itself.

## Emerging Trend: An Extended Plateau and the Shift Toward December 2026

The October 28 hold is better read as a waypoint than a turning point. In the simulation, the more important development is that the 2.25% plateau keeps extending while the policy debate moves forward to the **December 9, 2026** meeting and the first months of 2027. On October 28, forecasters are no longer asking whether the Bank acts. They are asking how much longer it can wait. This chapter follows that shift.

**A plateau that reaches one full year**

An eighth consecutive hold would leave the overnight rate unchanged for a full year after the last cut. The simulated record sets this against a long and eventful cycle:

> "The Bank of Canada last raised its overnight-rate target on 12 July 2023, to 5.00%, and held it through 10 April 2024; it then cut the rate at seven consecutive decisions from 5 June 2024 through 12 March 2025, reaching 2.75%, held in April, June, and July 2025, and cut by 25 basis points on 17 September 2025 to 2.50% and on 29 October 2025 to 2.25%."

The plateau has now lasted roughly as long as the 2023–24 hold at 5.00%. The difference is what the market is watching. During that earlier hold, the debate was about when cuts would start. In the simulated autumn of 2026, the forecasters who look past October are mostly debating when hikes start, even though the domestic economy is weakening.

**Forecast horizons are moving to "end of 2026" and "early 2027"**

Most forecasters in the record frame their views around the end of 2026 or early 2027, not around October. Three statements show how far out the horizon now sits:

> "After the 19 October 2026 CPI release, TD's Leslie Preston said the data supports the view that the Bank of Canada can remain on hold in 2026."

> "On 19 October 2026, CIBC's Andrew Grantham said Bank of Canada policymakers had time before raising rates, although a first hike could come before January 2027, when Andrew Grantham had forecast it would occur."

> "On 19 October 2026, Douglas Porter said the CPI report did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes."

Together these describe the range of expected paths:

- **TD:** holds through the rest of 2026, which would mean holds in both October and December.
- **CIBC:** a first hike pencilled in for January 2027, with an explicit warning that it could come sooner. December is the only scheduled meeting before January, so "sooner" effectively means December 9.
- **BMO:** sees no new case for tightening in the latest data, which leaves the plateau open-ended.

As covered earlier, the Reuters poll of October 21–23 found that 15 of 34 economists did not forecast a Bank of Canada hike. That figure is subject to the data-mixing caveat already discussed. Read as directional evidence, it suggests a majority of the panel expects tightening at some point over the forecast horizon, with October not among the likely dates. The timing debate is therefore concentrated in the December-to-early-2027 window.

**Why December becomes the contested meeting**

Very little scheduled data was left before October 28. By contrast, the six weeks between October 28 and December 9 are full of new information. The record lists the key releases:

> "The next jobs report is scheduled for 6 November 2026, after the decision mentioned in the source text."

> "The next CPI is scheduled for 16 November 2026, after the decision mentioned in the source text."

The scenario also expects a summary of the Governing Council's deliberations about two weeks after October 28. The previous precedent was a September 16 release covering the September 2 decision. Before December 9, then, markets will have:

1. **The October Monetary Policy Report and Macklem's 10:30 a.m. press conference with Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers.** Both come on October 28 itself, and the MPR is expected to raise the inflation forecast. Macklem had already said that "if oil prices stayed around US$100, inflation was expected to gradually rise from 3 per cent."
2. **The October deliberations summary**, which should show whether any members argued for an immediate hike.
3. **The October jobs report (November 6)**, which will show whether the August–September losses of 42,000 and 68,000 jobs continue or reverse.
4. **The October CPI report (November 16)**, the first test of whether the energy shock is spreading into core measures. Survey evidence released October 19 already showed "about half of firms planned to pass on cost increases."

This sequence is the main reason December looks less certain than October. The October call is anchored by data that is already known. The December call depends on data that does not yet exist.

**Global peers are already tightening**

The extended plateau also leaves the Bank increasingly out of step with other central banks. The simulation records tightening abroad:

> "The US Federal Reserve lifted its range to 3.75%-4.00% on 16 September 2026."

> "The ECB raised its rates effective 17 June 2026 and again effective 16 September 2026."

Analysts expect more European tightening. "Desjardins switched to an ECB rate-hike call on 19 October 2026." In the Reuters poll, "16 of 29 economists expected another ECB rate hike by the end of March 2027." Scotiabank's Derek Holt described the next ECB move as "not a slam-dunk call but that there was probably enough supporting evidence for a hike."

The knowledge graph also contains an entry stating that "the Bank raised its rate range to 3.75%-4.00% on 16 September 2026." That entry is inconsistent with the Bank of Canada's single 2.25% target, and its numbers match the Fed's action exactly. We treat it as a mislabeled Fed fact, not as evidence of a Canadian move.

The gap between a Fed range of 3.75–4.00% and a Bank of Canada target of 2.25% is the background to the "when to hike" debate. In the final week, the currency moved in a direction that reduces pressure on the Bank somewhat:

> "The Bank of Canada's daily rate was 1.3993 Canadian dollars per US dollar on 23 October 2026, compared with 1.4097 Canadian dollars per US dollar on 19 October 2026."

A modestly stronger loonie dampens imported inflation at the margin. That makes it slightly easier for the Bank to wait one more meeting, though it does not end the debate.

**Weak growth is what keeps the plateau in place**

The opposing force is growth. The Reuters panel expects the economy to slow into year-end:

> "Economists polled by Reuters on 21–23 October 2026 estimated Canadian GDP growth of about 1.9% in the third quarter of 2026 and 1.3% in the fourth quarter."

Macklem's own warning was more pessimistic than the panel's consensus:

> "On 21 September 2026, Governor Tiff Macklem said that if new US tariffs stay in place, fourth-quarter growth could be roughly halved to below 1%."

The Governing Council had also concluded before September 2 that "the economy was still in excess supply." Rising unemployment, wage growth of only 2.3%, fourth-quarter growth possibly below 1%, and spare capacity together create a strong institutional reason to keep holding. In effect, the plateau is the Bank's way of balancing an energy-driven inflation shock against a tariff-driven growth shock without committing to either side.

**What this means for the forecast**

The trend reinforces our October call and makes December the meeting to watch. October is the last meeting at which forecasters can say "wait" without first seeing new evidence. December 9 is the first meeting at which the CIBC-style "could come before January" risk can actually materialise. The simulated record contains no explicit market probability for December, so we do not assign one. Qualitatively, December is clearly more contested: the TD view (hold through 2026) and the early-hike CIBC risk case now apply to the same meeting.

Several signposts from October 28 onward will indicate whether the plateau continues into 2027 or ends in December:

- **MPR inflation path:** how far the projection rises above 3% under current oil prices of US$113–117.
- **Statement language:** whether the conditional "prepared to adjust monetary policy as needed" becomes more explicit guidance toward tightening.
- **Deliberations summary:** whether any members argued for an October hike.
- **November 6 jobs and November 16 CPI:** whether job losses continue, which would extend the plateau, or core measures move back above 2%, which would bring forward the first hike.

A note on sources: as in the previous chapter, the agent interview environment was unavailable. This trend analysis is based only on the recorded facts in the simulated knowledge graph, not on first-person agent testimony.
