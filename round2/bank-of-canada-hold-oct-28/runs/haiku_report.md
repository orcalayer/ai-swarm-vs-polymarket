# Future Forecast Report: Bank of Canada Hold at 2.25% on October 28, 2026

> The simulated future points strongly to the Bank of Canada leaving its overnight rate unchanged at 2.25% on October 28, 2026, with consensus expectations of a hold and few credible signals of a move.

---

## Baseline Outcome: A Hold Is the Dominant Path

The simulated future points most strongly to the Bank of Canada leaving its overnight rate at 2.25% on October 28, 2026. The baseline path is a hold, and the evidence for it comes mainly from the forecasters and the latest inflation data rather than from any official signal of intent.

**The starting point is a long hold**

The target has not moved since 29 October 2025. The simulated record notes that "The Bank of Canada kept its rate at 2.25% at seven consecutive decisions after the rate reached 2.25% on 29 October 2025; the latest of those decisions was on 2 September 2026." A further hold on October 28 would therefore extend an already established pause rather than break one.

**Forecasters expect no move**

The most direct evidence is the set of institutional forecasts gathered around the 19 October CPI release. Several banks and economists expected no change:

> "On 19 October 2026, Peter Shannon expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move."

> "On 19 October 2026, RBC expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move."

> "On 19 October 2026, Sherry Cooper expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move."

Continuum Economics also expected a hold on 21 October 2026, and Standard Chartered expected one on 20 October 2026. The Reuters poll is more mixed but still does not point to a hike at this meeting: "Of 34 economists surveyed, 15 did not forecast a Bank of Canada rate hike." The simulation does not record a majority forecasting a hike on October 28, and it records a number of economists explicitly expecting no move.

**Inflation data supports waiting**

The 19 October CPI release reduced the pressure for tightening. Headline CPI rose 3.1% year over year in September, slightly below the 3.2% consensus, and core measures eased. CPI-trim fell to 1.8% from 1.9%, and CPI-median eased to 1.9% from 2.0%. TD's economist described the implication directly:

> "After the 19 October 2026 CPI release, TD's Leslie Preston said the data supports the view that the Bank of Canada can remain on hold in 2026."

BMO's Douglas Porter took a similar line, saying "the CPI report did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes." CIBC's Andrew Grantham said policymakers "had time before raising rates," with a first hike possibly before January 2027, which places any move well after the October decision.

**Hawkish signals are real but do not dominate**

The simulation does contain upside risks. Governor Tiff Macklem said on 17 October that the Bank expected to raise its inflation forecast because oil prices were far above its July assumption, and that "the risk of inflation broadening had increased." Some forecasters have shifted toward hikes abroad, including BMO and Desjardins switching to ECB hike calls on 19 October. These signals argue for vigilance but are not the Bank of Canada's own guidance for a move on October 28.

Labour data is soft in the other direction. The Labour Force Survey of 9 October reported that employment fell by 42,000 in August and 68,000 in September, while unemployment rose to 6.5%. This weakens the case for a hike, though the simulation records no economist explicitly arguing for a cut at this meeting; one user comment noted that a cut "looked remote" because no forecast reviewed from 9 to 23 October called for one.

**Resolution mechanics reinforce the hold**

The resolution rules also favour a YES outcome. A cancelled announcement, or no decision before the 9 December 2026 scheduled decision, counts as no change. A postponed decision counts only if it is made before 9 December. Any move of 25 basis points or more, or even 10 basis points rounded up, counts as NO, so the bar for a change is clear.

**Assessment**

The simulated evidence consistently points toward a hold: a seven-decision pause, explicit no-change calls from several major forecasters, softening core inflation, and no official signal of a move. The main risks to this baseline come from oil-driven inflation, the Bank's own upside inflation warning, and the hawkish drift abroad. The simulation does not show these becoming decisive by the October 28 announcement, so the hold is the dominant path.

## How Economists, Banks and Officials Reacted

**Official communication: the Governor is signalling vigilance, not a move**

The Bank's own officials have said little about the October 28 decision itself, but their recent remarks set the tone. On 17 October, speaking at the IMF meetings in Bangkok, Governor Tiff Macklem stated that the Bank expected to raise its inflation forecast:

> "On 17 October 2026, Tiff Macklem said the Bank of Canada expected to raise its inflation forecast because oil prices were far above the Bank of Canada's July assumption."

He also flagged broadening risks and a conditional path for inflation:

> "On 17 October 2026, Tiff Macklem said the risk of inflation broadening had increased."

> "On 17 October 2026, Tiff Macklem said that if oil prices stayed around US$100, inflation was expected to gradually rise from 3 per cent."

On the growth side, his earlier remark on 21 September was more cautious:

> "On 21 September 2026, Governor Tiff Macklem said that if new US tariffs remain in place, fourth-quarter growth could be roughly halved to below 1%."

Taken together, the Governor's comments describe a balance of upside inflation risk and downside growth risk. They do not announce a hike or a cut. The simulation also records no statement from Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers beyond her scheduled role at the press conference, so there is no separate signal from her on the decision.

The Bank's 2 September decision set the same framing. The simulation records that it "said on 2 September 2026 that it was prepared to adjust monetary policy as needed" and that "the upside risks to inflation had increased." The Bank will publish the new Monetary Policy Report alongside the October 28 decision, and the Governor and Rogers will hold a press conference at about 10:30 a.m. ET, so the reaction to the Bank's own wording will arrive on the day.

**Bank economists: hold is the consensus, with hike risk noted**

Major Canadian bank economists largely expected no change, as the baseline chapter documents. Their reactions after the October 19 CPI release add nuance:

- **RBC** kept its hold call after the data, with a forecast still in place on 22 October.
- **Standard Chartered** and **Continuum Economics** expected a hold on 20 and 21 October respectively.
- **CIBC's Andrew Grantham** framed the timing as a question of patience: policymakers "had time before raising rates," though he said a first hike could come before January 2027.
- **BMO's Douglas Porter** was the most direct in dismissing the case for tightening, saying the CPI report "did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes."

Hike calls are concentrated abroad. The simulation records BMO switching to hike calls on 19 October, and Desjardins switching to an ECB rate-hike call the same day. **Scotiabank's Derek Holt** was measured on that front, saying an ECB hike "was not a slam dunk call but that there was probably enough supporting evidence for a hike." These views are about foreign central banks rather than the Bank of Canada, so they add a hawkish backdrop without changing the October 28 baseline.

**Market-wide survey: a split on the wider direction, not on this meeting**

The Reuters poll of 21 to 23 October gives a sense of the spread. Of 34 economists, 15 did not forecast a Bank of Canada hike, while a separate ECB question found 19 of 34 expecting an ECB increase to 2.50%. Economists polled also projected Canadian GDP growth of about 1.9% in the third quarter and 1.3% in the fourth. Slower growth and a softer labour market, with employment down 42,000 in August and 68,000 in September, give those who lean dovish a reason to see the policy rate as restrictive enough.

**Interview evidence was not obtained**

The planned agent interviews could not be run. The tool reported that the simulation environment was not running and returned no interview records. This section therefore relies on the forecasts, official remarks and survey results above, not on first-person responses from simulated officials or economists. No cut was called for by any forecaster reviewed in the 9 to 23 October window, so the reaction set is centred on hold, with hike risk coming from abroad and from the Governor's inflation warnings rather than from a stated Bank of Canada plan.

## Risks and Tail Scenarios That Could Break the Hold

The baseline hold could be broken in two directions. An energy-driven inflation surprise could push the Bank toward a hike, and a sharp labour or growth deterioration could push it toward a cut. The simulated evidence supports the first risk more than the second, but neither is strong enough on the current record to override the hold.

**The upside tail: an oil-led inflation broadening**

The most important upside risk is energy. The simulation records that the Bank expected its inflation forecast to rise because oil prices were far above its July assumption, and Governor Tiff Macklem added a conditional path:

> "On 17 October 2026, Tiff Macklem said that if oil prices stayed around US$100, inflation was expected to gradually rise from 3 per cent."

> "On 17 October 2026, Tiff Macklem said the risk of inflation broadening had increased."

Oil has remained well above that level. The simulation records that "Reuters reported oil at US$117 on 23 October 2026," and that "the Hub reported that Brent settled at US$113.17 on 22 October 2026." The Bank's own July assessment had put the peak in context: "The Bank of Canada wrote on 29 July 2026 that benchmark oil had peaked near US$120 a barrel in April 2026." The simulation links this to the Iran war, which "disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz."

Expectations are another route to a hike. Surveys released on 19 October found that "about half of firms planned to pass on cost increases, while nearly a quarter of firms could not say when inflation would return to 2%," and that "consumers' long-term inflation expectations" were rising. If those signals strengthened, the Bank's argument for tightening would become more concrete.

The simulation also shows why this route is unlikely to trigger on 28 October. Before the 2 September decision, the Governing Council "saw little evidence that gasoline costs were passing through to other prices," and it judged that "the economy was still in excess supply." The 19 October CPI data then narrowed the core measures, with CPI-trim at 1.8% and CPI-median at 1.9%. An energy shock would need to show up in core inflation before the Bank moved, and the simulated data do not yet show that.

**The downside tail: a labour and trade shock that leads to a cut**

The downside scenario rests on the labour market and trade. The Labour Force Survey of 9 October showed that "employment fell by 68,000 in September 2026, following a 42,000 drop in August," and that "unemployment rose to 6.5%." Trade is a second pressure point, since after "Canada-US trade talks broke down on 21 August 2026, the United States Government imposed new 50% tariffs affecting about US$20 billion of Canadian goods." Governor Macklem had earlier warned that "if new US tariffs remain in place, fourth-quarter growth could be roughly halved to below 1%."

A cut is the less likely outcome in the simulation. A user comment noted that "a rate cut looked remote because no forecast reviewed from 9–23 October 2026 called for one, although job losses, tariffs, and sub-2% core inflation would be arguments for a cut." The Reuters poll also projected growth of "about 1.9% in the third quarter of 2026 and 1.3% in the fourth quarter," which is weaker but not a recession call. The labour weakness is therefore a reason for patience rather than an immediate easing trigger on this date.

**Market stress as a potential accelerant**

The simulation records relatively orderly currency moves. The Bank of Canada's daily rate "was 1.3993 Canadian dollars per US dollar on 23 October 2026, compared with 1.4097 Canadian dollars per US dollar on 19 October 2026," which means the loonie strengthened over that span. No bond-market dislocation or financial-stress event appears in the retrieved records. Without a disorderly move, market pressure is unlikely to force an out-of-cycle change, and the rules in any case ignore emergency moves outside the 28 October announcement.

**Timing risk: a move deferred rather than advanced**

Even where pressure builds, the timing favours a later move over an immediate one. Forecasters placed the next possible hike well after this meeting. CIBC's Andrew Grantham said policymakers "had time before raising rates," with a first hike possibly before January 2027. The next scheduled decision after this one is 9 December 2026, which is also the outer limit for a postponed announcement to count as a decision. A hike at that later meeting would still resolve NO for the 28 October question only if it applied to the target that day. As the simulation describes it, a decision made after 28 October would not change the outcome for this question unless the announcement were postponed and then made before 9 December.

**Assessment**

The simulated tail risks are real but asymmetric. An oil-driven inflation broadening, supported by firm inflation expectations and oil above US$110, is the most credible route to a change, and it would most likely surface in the Monetary Policy Report and press conference on 28 October as a hawkish signal rather than an immediate move. A growth-led cut is weakly supported, since no reviewed forecast called for one. Market stress and mechanical postponement risks appear minor in the retrieved records. These tails do not overturn the baseline hold, but they explain why the hold is a strong expectation rather than a certainty.

## Probability Assessment and Forward Trends

**Probability estimate: 92 out of 100 that the Bank of Canada leaves the target for the overnight rate at 2.25% on October 28, 2026.**

This estimate rests on the simulated evidence gathered across the forecasts, official remarks, data releases and resolution rules. No market-implied probability, such as overnight index swap pricing, appeared in the retrieved records, so the figure is a judgment built from the simulated record rather than a reading of market odds.

**Why the hold is strongly favoured**

The simulated record contains several converging signals. The Bank has held at 2.25% through seven consecutive decisions, and the simulation records no official signal of an imminent move. Named forecasters expected no change: "On 19 October 2026, RBC expected the Bank of Canada to make no rate move," and the same was recorded for Peter Shannon and Sherry Cooper. Standard Chartered, Continuum Economics and RBC again expected a hold on 20, 21 and 22 October. In the Reuters poll, "Of 34 economists surveyed, 15 did not forecast a Bank of Canada rate hike," which means the poll does not show a hike consensus for this meeting.

The inflation data also moved away from tightening. The CPI report of 19 October showed "CPI-trim at 1.8%, CPI-median at 1.9%, CPI excluding gasoline at 2.2%, and headline CPI at 3.1%, below the 3.2% consensus." BMO's Douglas Porter said the report "did nothing to advance the case for interest rate hikes."

**Why the remaining probability is not higher**

The 8-point gap reflects the hawkish risks that the simulation does record. The Governor said "the risk of inflation broadening had increased," and oil prices remained high, with "Reuters reported oil at US$117 on 23 October 2026." Bank surveys showed "consumers' long-term inflation expectations rising." These are the signals most capable of shifting the Bank toward a hike, and they are not yet visible in core inflation.

The downside is less likely to produce a move on this date. A rate cut would need a forecast call, and the simulation records that "no forecast reviewed from 9–23 October 2026 called for one." Weak employment, with "employment fell by 68,000 in September 2026," supports patience rather than an immediate cut.

**Indicative scenario weights**

- **Hold at 2.25%:** about 92 percent. This is the central case, supported by the forecasts, the core inflation trend and the Bank's own framing of "upside risks to inflation" balanced against weak growth.
- **Hike of 25 basis points or more:** about 6 percent. This would require the Bank to treat the oil-driven inflation outlook as more urgent than the core data suggest. The simulation records BMO and Desjardins moving to ECB hike calls, which points to a hawkish environment abroad rather than a Canadian call.
- **Cut of 25 basis points or more:** about 2 percent. This would require a sharp labour or trade deterioration that the simulated forecasters had not yet priced.

Under the resolution rules, a postponed decision made before December 9 counts on its merits, while cancellation or no decision before that date counts as YES. These mechanics add a small amount of support to the YES outcome.

**Forward trends to watch**

- **The October 28 Monetary Policy Report and press conference.** The Bank will publish the new report alongside its decision at 9:45 a.m. ET, and Governor Tiff Macklem and Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers will hold a press conference at about 10:30 a.m. ET. Any shift in the inflation outlook language, particularly around "the risk of inflation broadening," is the most likely source of a late reassessment.
- **Oil and energy pass-through.** The simulation links the inflation forecast to oil staying near US$100 or above. Continued pressure would keep the hawkish case alive, while a fall would strengthen the hold.
- **Inflation expectations.** The simulation records firms planning to pass on cost increases, and nearly a quarter of firms unable to say when inflation would return to 2%. Rising expectations are the mechanism by which an energy shock could become a hike argument after this meeting.
- **Labour and growth.** Unemployment rose to 6.5%, and the Reuters poll projected growth of about 1.9% in the third quarter and 1.3% in the fourth. Further deterioration would weaken the hike case and begin to build a cut argument ahead of December 9.
- **The December 9 decision.** The simulation places the next scheduled decision on December 9, 2026, with the summary of deliberations for October expected about two weeks after the announcement. That summary may show how close the Governing Council came to a move.

**What would change the estimate**

A hawkish shift would come from a clear hike signal in the Governor's remarks, a rise in core CPI measures, or a Reuters or bank poll that moves toward a majority hike call for October. A dovish shift would come from a new forecast calling for a cut, or a further sharp drop in employment. In the simulated record available so far, neither has appeared, so the hold remains the dominant path.
